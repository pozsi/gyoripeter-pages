2014-08 Homelessness report Hungary 2013

Péter Győri: Homelessness Report - 2013[1]

The year 2013 – without a doubt – is going to be remembered as the “year of the criminalization of homelessness” in the long history of homeless policies in Hungary.

In March 2013, the Parliament voted to include the infamous fourth amendment of the Constitution, stating that “for the protection of public order, public safety, public health and cultural heritage a law or local decree might outlaw occupying public space for living purposes”. Thus it annulled right of the ombudsman and the Constitutional Court, previously utilized, to question whether a law of this kind is constitutional.

Since October 15th 2013, based on the new Constitution, the Parliament modified the law on petty crime, outlawing “occupying public space for living purposes” on world heritage sites as well as areas declared by local authorities, punishable by public work, or, if the delinquent does not accept that, by a fine. If the person has been charged twice with the same petty crime within six months, they can be imprisoned. The act includes a paragraph on those living in huts: “If someone constructs a building on public land without the written consent of the owner or the person responsible for that land, he or she commits an offense”.

At about the same time, in November and December 2013 the City Hall of Budapest passed its own decree – in accordance with the new law on petty crime – and designated zones where “occupying public space for living purposes” is to be considered illegal.

Prior to this, in 2010, the Parliament had given the right to local authorities to decide what they consider to be an “improper use of public space”, then included the possibility of criminalizing “occupying public space for living purposes” as well as storing one’s belongings on public space in the modification of the law on petty crime, and the City Hall of Budapest voted on punishing rough sleeping as an offense in the inner districts of the City. In December 2011 the ombudsman asked the Constitutional Court to examine whether these bills and decrees were in accordance with the Constitution. In November 2012, the Constitutional Court decided that the paragraphs outlawing rough sleeping in the new law on petty crime were anti-constitutional, and thus annulled them.

These events hardly affected the lives of most homeless people, but excited NGOs working with homelessness and human rights both on a national and international level, as well as the public bodies involved and the national press. The majority of the population still thought that mass-homelessness should be solved primarily through offering support, employment and housing instead of repression (several independent surveys came up with the same result).

The lives of homeless people were more influenced in 2013 by the lack of work, lack of secure housing and diminishing social transfers. Only 7% of homeless people had regular work, 22% lived from casual work while 28% supported themselves from gathering, begging or garbage hunting. Every second homeless person received some sort or financial support (old age pension, disability pension, unemployment benefit or a benefit from the local government).[2]

The results of the annual survey also precisely reflect how the livelihood of homeless people in Hungary has radically diminished since 2007. Less and less people have access to employment, the income of the poorest has gotten smaller. A significant portion of homeless people (41%) have no idea of where they will live the following year. Every fifth homeless person thinks that they will never live in normal housing again.[3]

In a national representative survey 7% of the adult Hungarian population (560 thousand people) said that in their circle of relatives, acquaintances there is someone, who is homeless.[4]

9793 people took part voluntarily in the annual February 3rd survey of February 2013. 32% of them slept rough, 28% slept in a night shelter while 40% slept in hostels. The majority (5570 people) lived in the countryside, where their number was gradually increasing, while the rest (4223 people) lived in Budapest, where their number was stable, slightly decreasing. The level of schooling of homeless people was very low (48% only had 8 grades of elementary education at most), they were not young (57% was older than 50), the ratio of women was increasing (21%). In 2013, 13 000 different people used night shelters, 9 000 people slept in hostels and 33 000 people turned up day centres for the homeless. These latest services are not only used by “visible homeless” people (those sleeping rough or in night services), but also those living in housing poverty (in homes where the electricity has been cut, or where there is not enough money to buy food, etc).

Homelessness cannot be separated from insecure housing and housing poverty. In Hungary, households on average spend 25% of their income on housing. This rate is 46% in average among the poorest fifth of the population! 27% of households cannot heat their house normally during winter. An estimated 413 000 households are more than 90 days behind with paying their bills, while 810 000 households have debt arrears. The rate of those with arrears among those with the lowest income is far the highest among the EU countries, more than twice than that of the EU average, and 1.5 times more than that of the average of the CEE member states. According to Eurostat, 45.7% of the whole population, 70.1% of those with a low income and 78.1% of families with more than one child live in overcrowded housing. 2013 has seen the fewest number of housing units (7300) built in a 100 years – including the two world wars! The Law on Housing was modified several times in 2013, each modification further diminishing housing security. In Hungary, only 304 000 households out of the 4.1 million housing units officially rent their homes with a contract. Only 3% (120 000 housing units) of all housing is rented by local authorities, legal private rental is about 4%, while illegal private rental is estimated to be about another 4%. Hungary is one the last countries in the EU with regards to its rental housing stock, thanks to the forced privatization of housing.

Ψ

The Hungarian Constitution does not include the citizens right to housing in an accountable way. However, in its aforementioned fourth amendment it is declared that “Article 22 (1) Hungary shall strive to provide the conditions of housing according to human dignity as well as access to public services for all its citizens. (2) The state and local governments shall, among others, endorse the creation of the conditions of housing according to human dignity by striving to provide accommodation to all homeless citizens.” In Hungary local governments are obliged to provide care and rehabilitation to citizens who have become homeless in their territory, as well as make steps to prevent people becoming homeless. The Social Act states that “Local governments, without regards to their scope of authority or jurisdiction, are obliged to provide people in need with temporary financial assistance, food and accommodation, if the lack of these endanger the safety or livelihood of the person in need.” According to the annual Budget Act, “financial assistance can be provided [from the national budget] to support the housing of homeless people leaving temporary accommodation facilities”. However, none of these was applied by either the local or central government agencies concerned in 2013 (nor before that). For this reason, this report shall discuss what needs to be done in order to apply these regulations: prevent and reduce homelessness in Hungary.

Some tasks from 2013, just as examples:

  • The government should prepare a thorough and detailed strategy about how it wishes to provide “accommodation according to human dignity” for all its citizens, and make the necessary steps to achieve this strategy.
  • The government should prepare a thorough and detailed strategy about how it wishes to provide “accommodation to all homeless citizens”, and make the necessary steps to achieve this strategy.
  • The Parliament should create the financial and legislative environment to enable local governments to provide accommodation for all homeless citizens.
  • All local governments should prepare a midterm as well as a yearly plan about
  • taking care of homeless citizens in their area
  • the rehabilitation of homeless citizens
  • and the prevention of homelessness.
  • The definition of “homeless” (which at the moment only contains those without an address or sleeping rough) should be replaced by “houseless” in the Social Act: “A person is houseless if they cannot provide the minimal conditions of housing, defined by law, for themselves or for their families on their own. These persons are entitled to housing allowance for the houseless and, if needed, accommodation.” The minimal conditions of housing should be defined according to different target groups.
  • The system of central housing allowance should be modified and expanded to include prevention as well as to help those already homeless back to sustainable housing.
  • To limit the losses of households accumulating large debts, a law on “Personal bankruptcy” has to be developed.
  • A new regulation of “Secure tenancy” and the institution of “Social Rental Agencies” has to be introduced. Private renting should be encouraged by changing the regulations regarding its taxation, social renting to members of vulnerable groups should be regulated and supported in a new way. The privatization of local government housing should be stopped.

This report, among many other recommendations, declares that in order to solve the various tasks in relation to homelessness in Hungary, a firm, realistic long-term strategy, strong political and decision making will, a professional action plan, and, for their realization, detailed and precise channels of cooperation and negotiation are needed: in short, “good governance”.

(Translated by Boróka Fehér)

[2] Based on the 2013 survey of the independent February 3rd research group.

[3] Ibid.

[4] The survey, ordered by Menhely Foundation, was implemented by Median Institute of Opinion and Marketing Research, between March 22-26, 2013.
