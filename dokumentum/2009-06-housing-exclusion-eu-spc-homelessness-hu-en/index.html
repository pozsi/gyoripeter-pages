Questionnaire to Member States concerning homelessness and housing exclusion[1]

A. CURRENT SITUATION

1/ Has your Member States adopted a formal definition or typology for homelessness and housing exclusion? How does it relate to the ETHOS typology?  

For “housing exclusion”, Hungary has not accepted formal definition.

However, in Hungary the Act III of 1993 on social administration and social services has been in effect for 16 years, and it states

that

„(1) homeless people shall be persons without any registered place of residence, except for persons whose registered places of residence are accommodation for homeless people”,

and

„(2) any person shall be deemed to be homeless who spends nights in public areas or premises not designed for housing purposes”. 

In the light of these two definitions, the persons concerned may receive all the financial benefits and social services that are regulated by the Act, and also provided to other people in need. The status of homelessness as defined by the above-referenced Act is also considered in the provision of healthcare and free healthcare services.

According to the legal definition provided above, Hungary officially regards persons belonging to Category 1.1., 2.1., 3.1., 3.2., 3.3., 4.1., 7.1., 11.1., 11.2., 11.3., 12.1. of the ETHOS typology to be homeless people alongside with persons belonging to ETHOS Category 7.2 in view of certain supports and programmes.

2/ Please provide any information available on the extent and on the emerging trends for homelessness and housing exclusion (HHE) and any analysis on the profile of homeless people. In particular:

Since 1999, in Hungary a data survey routine is performed each year on the same date (on 3 of February) with the use of identical methods among homeless people – people living rough or accommodation for homeless people – in Budapest, and since 2005 in other large cities.[2] In 2007, a comprehensive study was also conducted in relation to the situation of institutions for the provision of services to homeless people, their employees and the homeless people relying on these services.[3]

On the basis of this information, on any winter day approx. 3,000 people sleep without shelter: half of them in Budapest, and others in various large cities. Approx. 9,000 people spend their night in various types of accommodation for homeless people.  According to the survey of 2009, the average proportion of people living rough or residing in accommodation for homeless people is 0.2 percent in the total population, and in larger cities it ranges between 0.1 and 0.39 percent.

The official census (including the registration of data on housing units) almost exclusively focuses on the population living in housing units, still the most reliable view on people without apartments, homes can be derived from the census. Please describe the significance and the main reasons for homelessness and housing exclusion in your country.

After the change of the political regime, in Hungary housing policy lost grounds, and primarily the programmes cutting the burdens on the budget were dominant (privatization of apartments, settlement of the interests of loans having been subsidized formerly), and institutional reforms not requiring direct budgetary expenses (establishment of institutions for the financing of housing) were on the agenda.

After 2000, the new housing policy set the objectives of establishing a financing system of housing based on the provision of loans, increasing the importance of lease apartment and in particular social housing, as well as the modernization of the existing apartments. The programme did have achievements in the commencement of the financing of housing, yet its economic, social efficiency can be largely doubted. The lease apartment programme came to a halt in 2004 without having considerable achievements, and then after 2004 the so-called prefab (renovation) programme was initiated.

Certain critical points of today’s housing situation are:

The affordability of housing costs represents serious problems for low-income groups, which is not solely associated with low incomes (unemployment, individual disadvantaged situations), but the differing costs of the various housing units (e.g. large costs carried by apartments in housing estates with district heating). In more than 20% of Hungarian households, the proportion of housing costs exceeds 30% of the volume of incomes. In 2003, cc. 500 thousand households had arrears threatening the security of housing, while only 180–200 thousand households received housing support.

The proportion of lease apartments owned by local governments dropped from 22% to 4% due to the privatization of apartments in the 1990s and the conditions of support–taxation–incentives, while the proportion of privately owned lease apartments was cc. 4%.  (Meaning that the proportion of privately owned apartments is outstandingly large within the European Union, i.e. 92%.) Thus, the traditional housing base of social housing policy narrowed (lease apartments of local governments), and on the other hand there emerged no comprehensive system to guarantee the security of housing, and manage social and housing policy elements in a standard manner.

Due to the opening gap between large apartment prices and incomes, the affordability of acquiring apartments at proper standards poses serious problems even on average families that cannot rely on the help of parents–relatives (the average price of apartments is 5–6 times larger than the average yearly income in comparison with the corresponding average 3–4-fold indicator in the European Union). Around 2006, by taking out a loan the average household could pay for 50–60% of an average apartment (four years before, this rate was only 20–30%), but in 2009 with the deterioration of the conditions of housing loans the affordability indicator of apartments started to worsen again.

10–12% of all the apartments are of substandard quality, without or just partly equipped with conveniences, or emergency apartments. In addition, more than 100 thousand people live at segregated colonies being hardly suitable for housing. Furthermore, serious structural problems are faced: housing estates, old and deteriorating districts, housing units in depopulating villages with disadvantageous infrastructure, or block-type enclosures being hardly suitable for housing call for different solutions.

The domestic public, actors of policy-making are largely and continuously concerned with the various forms of homelessness and housing exclusion. The number and presence of roofless people are shocking to the Hungarian public, while the government and local governments are trying to manage this situation with the support of primarily non-governmental organizations, street services. In the past two decades, Hungary has set up such a network for the provision of services to homeless people that has an aggregate capacity of nearly ten thousand, mostly with the use of funding from the government and the Municipality of Budapest, as well as certain legal regulations. Yet, the general public is also concerned with the problems of unauthorized dwellers, Roma segregation (“Gipsy colonies”), urban slums with outdated housing conditions, the difficulties of hundreds of thousand households in paying public utility fees, indebtedness to public utility companies, and as a novel element the payment difficulties of apartment buyers relying on foreign exchange loans.

The main reasons are varied for these groups. In general, it can be claimed that in Hungary the primary causes of homelessness and housing exclusion are: unemployment, those driven out from the primary economy, the high proportion of people with uncertain incomes in the black economy, the fragmentation and deficiencies of the social, healthcare, addictological service system, the lack of guaranteed housing support systems, the deficiencies in coordination of central and local policies.

B. OVERALL POLICY FRAMEWORK and GOVERNANCE

1/ Which public authorities (national, regional or local) are responsible for policies related to homelessness and which ones for housing exclusion policies – or for both?

 At national level, please specify which Ministries are involved in the design, implementation and monitoring of these policies.

Homelessness issues belong to the scope of responsibilities of the Ministry of Social Affairs and Labour coordinating social services and benefits. Its activities embrace the drafting/preparation of the legal regulations determining the operating conditions of the institutional system for homeless people (certain legal regulations may as well be issued by the Minister of Social Affairs and Labour), and in addition it makes proposals to the Government in relation to the orientation of development in the given field, as well as measures to be taken. The same organization is responsible for the implementation or coordination of these programmes, as well as financing them partly at the account of its budgetary sources. Under the commission of the Ministry, certain programmes are frequently implemented by two public foundations being active in the field of services to homeless people: Public Foundation for Homeless People and “Solidarity” Public Foundation for the Houseless and Homeless in Budapest. Programmes implemented by non-governmental organizations are monitored by the Ministry.

The regularity of the arrangement of the programmes, the cost-efficiency of the governmental funds used is monitored by the State Audit Office and the Governmental Supervisory Office.  

Within the meaning of the Social Act, provision of services to homeless people belongs to the scope of responsibilities of the settlement (local) and county governments, as well as the district government and Municipality of Budapest. Under the Social Act, local governments with populations over ten thousand people are obliged to operate daytime shelters for homeless people, whereas local governments with populations over thirty thousand people are obliged to operate night-time shelters and temporary accommodation for homeless people, and local governments with populations over fifty thousand people are to run street social services, too. The local governments perform these tasks partly from own, and partly from governmental resources. Nevertheless, nearly 50% of the services are run by non-governmental organizations – with the financial support of the local governments and the central government.

Housing matters belong to the scope of competence of another ministry, the Ministry of Local Governments performing tasks in relation to the operation of the system of local governments. Within this scope of competence, they are involved in matters concerning the existing housing units, the framing of the regulatory background of housing while the problems of housing exclusion do not belong to their tasks.

For all forms of ownership, the regulations pertaining to the letting out of apartments are set forth in Act LXXVIII of 1993 on certain rules concerning the leasing of apartments and other premises, as well as their alienation [hereinafter referred to as Lt.] .

  • Lt. obligates local governments to pass local regulations – within the framework of the Act – in relation to the various issues concerning the letting out of apartments. Beyond this, Lt. does not prescribe how local governments should provide for the letting out of apartments they own. It is the tasks of the leasing organization of certain local governments to conclude the rental contracts on the basis of the regulations of the respective local governments as owners. If there is any dispute between the lessor and the tenant, the given contract may be challenged at civil courts of justices where legal protection may as well be requested.
  • Lt. obligates none of the settlements in the country to put any local housing programme in place. The creation of such a programme is always dependent on the local decision.
  • Pursuant to Section (1) of Article 84 of Lt., the general assembly of the local government may as well decide to compile a list of names applying for apartments in order to let out apartments with respect to the given social circumstances. The local government concerned may regulate the rules of the acceptance of applications, registration and the fulfilment of registered applications.
  • In contrast with the earlier conditions, on the basis of the new provisions of Lt. having entered effect on 31 March 2006 (Section (1) of Article 3) no one may be excluded from the provision of a social lease apartment even if the given person has lost its property for any reason or arbitrarily occupied any apartment. In these cases, the local government should examine only the income–property status.
  • Under Section (4) of Article 23 of Lt., local governments are obliged to provide for so-called temporary accommodation if the housing unit has been destroyed by the act of God or any other reason.

On the basis of the division of work within the government, the local housing support and the debt management programme of local governments belong to the scope of competence of the ministry involved in social matters, and they are to be operated pursuant to the so-called Social Act.

2/ What are the main national objectives and goals in terms of support for homeless people and access to housing?

Have specific targets at national or sub-national levels been adopted in terms of both outcomes and policy instruments/resources? Please provide the exact formulation of these targets.

Upon the request of the Prime Minister’s Office, in 2007 the first National Strategy for Homeless People was elaborated as an expert document of.[12] The key objectives set in the strategy were:

  • Prevention of homelessness, primarily with the means designed to strengthen the security of housing.
  • Transformation of the financing and legal regulations of the institutional system for the provision of services to homeless people towards result-oriented operations and the reintegration of homeless people.
  • The transformation of the operation of the current institutions for homeless people partly for the encouragement of admission of people living rough, and partly for the facilitation of housing and employment reintegration.

This document still has not been accepted by the government, but on the basis of the expert document in 2008 the Municipality of Budapest accepted its Strategy for Homeless People. Within this context, the Municipality of Budapest has concluded contracts for terms of several years with 12 major organizations providing services to homeless people in relation to the continuous operation of accommodation units, as well as with 7 non-governmental organizations in relation to the continuous provision of special services (crisis cars, dispatcher services, job-seeking offices, social care centres, etc.).  

In 2007, the Government approved a resolution that specifies certain tasks in connection with homelessness in the period of 2007–2013 alongside with the respective responsible institutions.

The document specifies the following measures:

  • extension of housing opportunities outside institutions (lease apartments, rentals) that are affordable for homeless people,
  • commencement of programmes for the promotion of the employment, housing of homeless people with the funding of the European Union,
  • similarly, with the use of EU sources, commencement of programmes that are designed for the review, transformation of the current institutional services, with particular respect to the provision of services to homeless people in need of special services (e.g. those leaving state care, with psychiatric diseases, addictions), as well as the development of the available institutional infrastructure,
  • expansion of capacities in low-threshold institutions that are operated at traffic nodes for people permanently living rough,
  • review of the current institutional services in order to render services that are better tuned for the demands, needs of those permanently living rough,
  • extension of services and capacities providing healthcare to homeless people, with special respect to healthcare centres operated in 24 hours,
  • popularization of the use of card-based consumption meters for the prevention of homelessness,
  • limitation of opportunities for eviction, stipulation of stronger conditions,
  • review of the housing support system for a broader scope of persons being eligible for rental subsidies.

For some of the objectives, quantified indicators have been specified:

  • a scheme should be worked out for the establishment and operation of external accommodation consisting of 500 apartments (to be leased to homeless people at affordable prices),
  • such a project should be launched with the use of EU funding and within the framework of the New Hungary Development Plan that offers the opportunity for the promotion of the employment and external housing of 3,500 homeless people,
  • the establishment of healthcare centres providing 7/24 services to homeless people in each of the 7 regions,
  • within the framework of the ministries concerned, it is to be examined how 300 hospital beds providing nursing and the attendance of chronic diseases to homeless people can be put in place.

3/ Please describe the overall policy framework devoted to preventing and combating homelessness and housing exclusion. In particular:

- Is priority given to certain specific groups (e.g. disabled people, families with children, the young and the elderly, Roma, immigrants…) or do you have horizontal overarching policies on HHE or both?  

- Is the gender dimension take into account when formulating policies in this area?  

- Is there a dedicated national budget for these policies and to how much does it amount? Please highlight the use of EU structural funds where relevant.

The intents of the Government have been described in Section 2. Key objectives in relation to the topic:

  • elaboration, execution of measures serving the prevention of homelessness,
  • review of the contents of services towards more efficient services to homeless people in need of special services, as well as the broadening of the scope of services rendered to homeless people permanently living rough, or homeless people who are not able or willing to rely on institutional services,
  • reduction of the number of homeless people living rough,
  • promotion of employment and housing towards successful social reintegration.

The particular gender priorities are not reflected in these concepts as specific priorities, yet such phenomena are considered as the fact that the majority of people living without any shelter belong to men, though the proportion of women has shown an increasing tendency in the past 2 years.

Separate governmental actions pertain to the prevention and efficient handling of violence against women and domestic violence, whose regulatory background is given by a national strategy approved by the Parliament in 2003.

Data in relation to people having suffered domestic violence can be queried from police statistics, as well as the statistical data of institutions assisting these people (family support and child welfare services, temporary homes of families, crisis telephone services) – yet, no comprehensive study has been conducted in this field.

Larger support programmes – housing exclusion

The following programmes are connected to the prevention and management of housing exclusion:

  • Prefab programme
  • Segregated site programme (eradication of the segregated colonies)
  • Support to obstacle-free access (programme for people with disabilities)
  • Social urban regeneration programme

The aim of the so-called prefab programme is to facilitate the energy-saving renovation of the buildings of housing estates having been built with the given industrial technology.  This way, the heating costs of apartments can be reduced, and therefore the maintenance costs of low-income households can be cut. Since 2001, approx. 200 thousand apartments have been concerned in this support renovation scheme. In the period of 2001–2004, the volume of support expended on the prefab programme was HUF 6 billion, then in 2005 and 2006 HUF 14 billion and HUF 15 billion, respectively. In 2007, no call for applications was announced, unlike 2008. Another goal of the prefab programme is to prevent the process of segregation in housing estates, decelerate the process of young people and well-to-do leaving these areas.

Under the management of the Ministry of Social Affairs and Labour, in cooperation with the National Labour Fund, since 2005 the grant application scheme entitled “housing and social integration of people living at segregated colonies, colony-type environments” has been operated. In the first years, the programme supported the mitigation of segregation in small settlements (under 2,000 inhabitants) – then in 2008 settlements with populations under 15,000 became involved, as well –, the moving of dwellers to integrated environments, as well as the renovation of residential buildings, social integration in relation to housing integration: implementation of training and employment programmes with the assistance of non-governmental organizations. The programme primarily concerns strongly segregated sites inhabited by Roma people. In the period of 2005–2008, 31 settlements took part in the programme, and 25 of these settlements had populations under 2,000 people. Initially, only local governments could submit applications for the programme, yet since 2007 NGOs have also been permitted to apply. Within the framework of the programme for the eradication of segregated sites, so far a HUF 2.8 billion support has been furnished from national resources.    

Applications for the support to barrier-free access can be submitted by seriously disabled people for the development of technically barrier-free apartments.  The repayable support can be provided to cover the extra costs of the development of barrier-free access, in a limited amount, and since 2001 the amount of the support has been unchanged (annually, HUF 2–3 billion is furnished to these ends).

Within the framework of Regional Operational Programmes, programmes for the so-called “Integrated social urban regeneration” are implemented in six regions of the country, particularly in larger towns, and in this context those on the peripheries of the local property market and living at segregated residential sites may be integrated socially and in their environments. The target area of social city rehabilitation includes both physically and socially deteriorated areas (housing estates and conventional urban areas), and its target group is the disadvantaged population of the given area. Interventions should be implemented in clearly demarcated action areas (whose deterioration is to be justified with the use of certain indicators), and consist of programme elements both for the renovation of residential buildings and ESF-type human resource development (training, employment, community development, etc.) alongside with the renovation of public areas. Infrastructural and institutional developments are also supportable, if these developments are serving for the improvement of housing conditions and the improvement of communal functions.

In the 2007–2008 period of the action plan, it was experienced that towns showed moderate interests in the social urban regeneration grants (support demands were received for a total amount of HUF 7.5 billion, whereas the total limit for the grants was HUF 44.5 billion). Towns had the primary focus on the renovation of public areas and institutions. Beside EU programmes, since 2005 the Municipality of Budapest has also been operating a social city regeneration fund for district governments to apply. From the fund, so far two projects have been supported, but unfortunately in the past two years the call for applications has not been announced.

Who are the main stakeholders in your country involved in the fight against homelessness and what is their role? What kind of financial support is given to them? Please describe the processes in place to involve key stakeholders, including users, in policy development, monitoring and evaluation.

The provision of services to homeless people sees a particularly large number of non-governmental service providers involved. According to the 2007 data of CSO, 63.3% of the people provided in permanent residential institutions for homeless people and 53% of the people provided in institutions for temporary accommodation (temporary hostels, night shelters) are attended by non-governmental organizations (that do not belong to local governments, either), while in day care 72.3% belongs to these service providers. Besides several dozens of non-governmental organizations, associations, foundations that have key roles in the local provision of services, and operate just one or two services, several larger non-governmental institutions operating national or Budapest-based, regional networks are involved in the provision of services to homeless people. If the conditions set forth by the government are in place, the same amount of normative contributions are furnished to cover operating costs with respect to the given capacities and number of people attended.

In addition to the organizations providing services, significant roles are taken by the two public foundations of this field of activities:

  • „Solidarity” Public Foundation for the Houseless and Homeless in Budapest jointly founded by the Government and the Municipality of Budapest for the support of services in Budapest especially afflicted with homelessness (as well as the Central Hungarian Region including Budapest), and
  • Public Foundation for Homeless People founded by the Government, and playing a leading role in the organization of professional and pilot projects for the entire country, as well as managing grants for all the regions except for Central Hungary.

The activities of both foundations embrace the allocation of the development funds furnished by the founders via grant application schemes, as well as the framing, implementation and coordination of professional orientations, concepts, pilot programmes in relation to homelessness. It is especially important that in the advisory boards of both organizations the representatives of the providing organization are involved, and therefore they can have their says in all the issues and processes of decision-making in representation of the interest of the providers. By today, these public foundations have become the strategic partners of the Government, and regularly contribute to the associated decision-making process of the Government.

The two organizations work in close cooperation with each other, their experts hold regular consultations on professional issues, and as a consortium they jointly participate in the central project that is entrusted to create the fundaments of the methodological work of the grant application scheme to be announced for the promotion of the employment and independent housing of homeless people with the use of EU funds. 5/ Are there any legal guarantees in your Member State enabling citizens to satisfy or support their housing needs?  Does your Member State recognize an enforceable right to housing – in what way?  

The right to housing has long been the subject of a debate in Hungary. In this respect, a proposal has been drafted to request the position of the Constitutional Court in the issue. The decision of the body has stressed that the right to housing cannot be deducted form the Constitution.

Therefore, there is no legal guarantee in relation to housing in the Hungarian legal system. However, within a limited scope it is prescribed in the Social Act: “As irrespective of its scope of tasks and competence, the local government is obliged to provide people in need with temporary aids, meals and accommodation if the lack of the same would threaten the life, health of the people in need.”

5/ Are there national/ regional standards for ensuring minimum quality of accommodation for the homeless?

In Hungary, the types of social services that can be operated are regulated by the Social Act and the associated decrees of enforcement. In this respect, the two most wide-spread types of temporary accommodation for homeless people are: night shelters of homeless people and temporary accommodation of homeless people. Pursuant to the Act, both types should be operated in settlements with populations over 30 thousand by the local governments. The same legal regulations prescribe in many details the minimum services to be provided, as well as the so-called minimum material and personnel conditions.

Night shelters of homeless people:

Night shelters are in fact services that ensure the night resting of homeless people.

  • Night shelters are to provide for premises for night resting, cleaning, warming of food, meals, separation of sick persons, community occasions.
  • In any night shelter, a single residential room may accommodate up to twenty people.
  • At least one shower and one toilet for each of the sexes should be provided for fifteen dwellers.
  • In a single residential room, each dweller should have a residential area of at least four square meters.
  • Heating, lighting and hot water supply should be provided continuously.
  • Night shelters are to provide for towels for cleaning, conditions for the cleaning of clothes, secure keeping of personal items, first-aid equipment.
  • Night shelter should be kept open in the evening and at night for at least fourteen hours.
  • In practice, when the night is intended to be spent in the night shelter each evening admission is to be requested at the gate of the institutions, and the accommodation should be left each morning, at the time of closing.
  • Night shelters should provide for duty services in the opening hours with the engagement of skilled labour.
  • In the night shelters, at least four hours a day social work should be performed with the engagement of social workers.

Temporary accommodation for homeless people:

Temporary accommodation is in fact services that assist homeless people in their housing, social and mental problems, as well as ensure accommodation.

In the same temporary accommodation, continuously maximum 2 years may be spent (services can be provided for 1 year, and if the housing problem of the homeless person has not been solved, the term of stay in the temporary hostel can be extended with an additional year).

The minimum quality requirements to be fulfilled on a mandatory basis are identical to those of the night shelter with the only difference that

  • in a single residential room up to 15 people can be accommodated,
  • bed linen should also be provided,
  • the opening hours should last for at least 16 hours a day,
  • social work should be performed for at least six hours a day.

6/ Please describe whether there are policies in place to support the move from emergency accommodation to more permanent solutions and if they are successful.

Since 2005, backed by a resolution of the government, the two public foundations mentioned in Section 4, the „Solidarity” Public Foundation for the Houseless and Homeless in Budapest jointly founded by the Government and the Municipality of Budapest (in Budapest, and thus the Central Hungarian Region), and the Public Foundation for Homeless People founded by the Government (for all the regions except for Central Hungary) operate special supported housing programmes from central budgetary sources with the involvement of organizations providing services to homeless people. The purpose of this programme is to support the social integration of homeless people, as well as offer solutions for housing outside institutions.

Within the framework of the programme, those people can be granted the so-called Integration Support who

  • have been living in the homeless shelter continuously for at least 120 days, or
  • have been living in the streets, public areas, places not suitable for use as apartments for at least 30 days, and kept contacts with any of the services for homeless people, and
  • whose net monthly incomes do not exceed 150% of the minimum wage, and
  • undertake cooperation with the supporting institutions for the time of follow-up assistance

Such Integration Support can be provided for the

  • costs of independent housing
  • rental fees
  • fees of workers’ hostels
  • caution money.

In addition to the financial support of independent housing, parts of the programme are a 12-month follow-up assistance, cooperation between the social worker and the client, the promotion of reintegration in the place of residence and the labour market.

In the central region, annually HUF 100 million is expended on the support. With the use of the support, in 2006–2007 (the 2008 performance still has not been summarized) the independent housing of approx. 1,000 homeless people could be resolved. It was indeed a breakthrough in comparison with the earlier years. The initial experience earned in the programme has been very positive in the light of the feedbacks of the organizations: it has offered prospects for those having long been homeless or living rough, creates the conditions of exit from the service system and restart, offers the re-establishment of partner relations, reintegration on the labour market. 9/ Please describe the main characteristics and access requirements of the social housing sector. What is the role of social housing as provider of housing solutions to homeless people?

Housing provision and social housing

As it has been mentioned above (Section B/1), the regulations pertaining to the letting out of apartments are set forth in Act LXXVIII of 1993 on certain rules concerning the leasing of apartments and other premises, as well as their alienation [hereinafter referred to as Lt.] stating that

  • Under Section (4) of Article 23 of Lt., the local government is obliged to provide for so-called temporary accommodation only if the housing unit has been destroyed by the act of God or any other reason,
  • Lt. does not obligate the settlements in the country to put any local housing service programme in place.
  • On the other hand, Lt. obligates local governments to pass local regulations – within the framework of the Act – in relation to the various issues concerning the letting out of apartments: rental conditions, establishment of rental fees, termination of the rental, etc.

The rental fees of the apartments of local governments – in contrast with apartments of other ownership – cannot be subject to “free agreement” under the former regulations. The provision of Lt. in effect since 31 March 2006 has changed this situation, because now local governments may pass decrees in relation three types of rental fees: market-based, cost-covering and so-called social. Social rental fees can be established whenever the tenant – under the conditions of the local decree – is eligible for rental subsidies. Within the meaning of the Act, the local government is to finance the rental subsidies of the local government to be provided to the tenant of any apartment of the local government from its own revenues.

At the beginning of 2006, there was an attempt made to grow the very small sector of domestic lease apartments: the modification of Government Decree no. 12/2001 (Jan. 31) on governmental housing subsidies on 01 February 2006 allowed the tenants of certain lease apartments newly constructed by local governments or private entities to receive special central rental subsidies provided that the local governments concerned apply for such funds. In the light of the related experience, this action having been announced should be revised, but it still has not been effectuated.

10/ Are there national goals for ensuring social mix, avoiding the concentration of disadvantaged dwellers in specific areas?

As a so-called official “national goal”, the aim to avoid the regional concentration of disadvantaged population is not prescribed, and the same applies to the reduction of concentration in the segregated districts.

On the other hand, the Act on equal opportunities states that during the framing of housing conditions the artificial segregation of certain groups on the basis of any characteristic trait is not allowable (Section (3) of Article 26 of Act 2003/CXXV), yet no other provision has been created for the practical interpretation of this legal regulation.

The issue of the spatial concentration of the disadvantaged population should be handled in the Integrated Urban Development Strategies (IUDS) to be elaborated by the cities. At the present, IUDS should be prepared by cities that apply for the urban regeneration grants of the EU in the period of 2007–2013. A mandatory section of IUDS is the so-called anti-segregation plan. In the anti-segregation plan, the settlement is to demarcate areas (segregated districts) where the proportion of the disadvantaged population is outstandingly high [16], and then for these segregated districts a vision should be framed (whether on the long run, the given area is to be rehabilitated or terminated), while on the medium run an intervention strategy should be worked out. The strategy should aim at the mitigation of segregation in the given area by enhancing the qualification–employment level of the local population, and putting a mobilization plan in place. Interventions should specify a complex set of devices (training–employment, education, active social work, community development, etc.), and be implemented in partnership with non-governmental organizations. According to the plans, in the future the elaboration of IUDS will be prescribed statutorily for each of the cities and towns, and therefore the preparation of the anti-segregation plan may become obligatory for towns.

I        Services aiming at the prevention of the accumulation of arrears

In the current social service system, programmes to assist the payment of costs associated with housing had an insignificant role until 2004. The normative housing support introduced in 2004 substantially improved the situation of people in need, because in addition to the housing supports of local governments funded from municipal resources a support 90% of which was funded from the central budget was introduced, and as a result the number of supported people increased from 150 thousand to 305 thousand within 3 years, while the related expenses grew from HUF 3.5 billion to 16.5 billion.

The purpose of the normative housing support was to increase the security of housing, prevent the population from becoming indebted, the emergence of arrears.

The housing support serves the security of housing by preventive means: it provides continuous contributions to the payment of overheads, and therefore it potentially prevents arrears from accumulating.

The Social Act regulated three types of this benefit:

  1. Normative: it is to be provided by all the local governments, and may be granted to persons living in households where the monthly per capita income is not more than 150 % of the prevailing minimum old-age pension (HUF 42,750 at the present) provided that acknowledged monthly maintenance costs of their home exceed 20 % of the total monthly income of their households;
  2. Related to debt management service (to be provided during the same term): its aim is to enable the debtor to cover his housing expenses in addition to the repayment of arrears;
  3. Local (for equity): local governments may provide it for equity as a supplement and/or independent benefit.

All the three types may be supplied as cash benefits or in-kind benefits (credited to the account of the beneficiary, or in any other form, e.g. fuel). The allowances are disbursed by the local governments, and 90% of the amount of the support can be reimbursed from the central budget.

In 2009, the ratio used as the basis of the support, i.e. the amount of the confirmed monthly costs of home maintenance per square meter rose from HUF 425 to HUF 450, and therefore the amount of the benefit increased by 5.8% – in excess of the inflation rate forecast for this year.

The social-based gas and district heating subsidies have a similar function, but they are more special (linked with specific types of energies), and the mechanisms of the awarding and enforcement of subsidies are also different.

From 2005 to 2008, the price of gas nearly doubled, yet with the formerly used scheme of subsidization the costs of subsidies could not be financed any longer.  The establishment of this targeted subsidization system was also promoted by the fact that the increase of energy prices affected the budgets of less well-off households more strongly: as energy expenses made up 7% of the incomes of households on the average, in low-income groups this proportion was 10–14%.  

Compensations are established by the Hungarian State Treasury (MÁK), and credited to the accounts of beneficiaries by the service providers (as an in-kind benefit).

Here, the scope of beneficiaries is also broader than in the case of the housing support: such households are eligible for the support where the net monthly income per consumer unit does not exceed the 3.5-fold amount of the old-age pension (currently HUF 99,750), and within this scope four income categories have been defined with varied extents of subsidization. The incomes serving as the basis of eligibility should only be stated at the time of application, and the correctness of the given data is supervised by MÁK (with random sampling) in a follow-up manner.

Gas price subsidies are proportionate to consumption, and the support is provided up to 2000 m3 per year at the present – for large families 4000 m3 per year –, while the actual volume of the subsidy is dependent on the actual housing situation (type of heating). It is well targeted, because in terms of incomes the lower 20% of the households receive 60% of the subsidies, still much less than the housing support where the lower 10% receive 100% of the support.

In the light of the large direct budgetary burdens and the dropping gas prices, this form of support is being subject to review at the present.

II.        Services aiming at the consolidation of arrears

Debt management services are designed to provide follow-up assistance to those who have arrears in relation to their home maintenance expenses (public utility fees, rentals, condo fees, housing loans). Within the framework of these services – typically operated in family support centres –, three-element assistance is provided combining in-kind benefits and services:

  • Debt reduction support: cash benefit provided in relation to the repayment of debts – with mandatory own contributions – and disbursed to the creditors (the maximum amount is HUF 200 thousand – or HUF 400 thousand for housing loans);
  • Housing support: in order to enable the client to cover its current housing expenses in addition to the repayment of arrears;
  • Debt management counselling: in order to establish proper financial management practices, use supports appropriately, as well as for prevention, the debtor is obliged to act in cooperation with a debt management consultant (trained now in 120 hours).

The operation of these services is mandatory only in settlements with population over forty thousand, as well as in the districts of Budapest, otherwise it is dependent on the will of the local governments whether they start up these services (in the light of the most recent data, the services are available to the population of cc. 140 settlements, and they may be made available to small settlements in partnerships).

The allowances are disbursed by the local governments, and 90% of the amount of the debt reduction and housing support can be reimbursed from the central budget.

III.        Other actions (not in the social sector)

Recently, the category of consumers to be protected has been introduced to the relevant legal regulations within the group of electric power and gas consumers: consumers socially in need and with disabilities. One of the specific allowances of these consumers – in addition to the option to pay in instalments or with grace periods – is the so-called pre-paying meter (card-based consumption meter) that can be an efficient device to preserve housing:

  • with the use of this meter, consumers cannot accumulate arrears, because they consume as much as they pay in advance (the pre-purchased volumes of energy will be charged to the equipment like in the case of card mobile telephones). Disconnection from the services can be avoided, and therefore the consumer is not to pay costly disconnection and reconnection fees;
  • As it can be monitored how much energy the persons concerned can still consume, the person economizes better, and therefore it encourages conscious and economical practices.

The procurement and installation costs of the equipment are covered by the service providers.

In the event of any default or non-payment, the consumer to be protected can be disconnected from energy supply only if – upon the recommendation of the service provider – the consumer does not agree with the installation of the special meter, or expressly hinders or prevents the installation, commissioning of the meter.

The programme described in Act IV of 2009 on the state joint and several guaranty of housing loans has been designed to support citizens who took out housing loans, but found themselves in  difficult situations due to the crisis: for a temporary term (2 years), the State of Hungary undertakes joint and several guaranty for those who took out housing loans described in the relevant legal regulations, and then in association with the crisis – beyond their own control – lost their jobs after 30 September 2008.

The government plans further actions towards the assistance of households that have become threatened by the crisis, yet the actual forms of these measures are still not known.

D. QUALITY OF HOUSING

1/ Please describe the policies to promote and safeguard the provision of adequate housing.  Is there a legal definition of "inadequate"/ "unfit for habitation" and "overcrowding"? Are there national goals or standards for ensuring minimum quality of housing?

In addition to the ones described in the other sections, no further measures are existent in relation to the promotion and provision of proper housing.

Both the Housing Act and the technical–construction legal regulations define the scope of constructed structures that can be deemed to be apartments, structures being suitable for housing purposes. The census (that also aims at the registration of housing conditions) is concerned with data who and how many people live in non-housing units, structures being unfit for housing purposes and where. (The results have been described above.)

At the present, there is no official legal definition for “overcrowding”. There used to be such a definition in the legal regulations on housing, where any situation where more than 2 people lived in the same residential room was deemed to be “overcrowded” – at the present, it is still the general approach.

National goals or standards for ensuring minimum quality of housing have not been specified.

2/ Please specify the links between policies to promote adequate housing and other urban regeneration policies (concerning local economic development, public transport, local public services…), especially for the most deprived neighbourhoods.

In general, it can be stated that currently there is no close link between the actions for the promotion of appropriate housing and measures for other urban regeneration. In spite of this fact, the prescribed topics of the above-mentioned Integrated Urban Development Strategy includes this aspect, especially in the requirement pertaining to the anti-segregation plan, but the associated practical effects can be evaluated only at a later date, because the strategies were framed during 2008. The purpose of social urban regeneration programmes is to make deteriorated areas integral parts of cities, and improve the access of the local population to services. Nevertheless, the projects that have been elaborated so far reflect these aspects only to a minor extent on the level of actual interventions.  

E. HOMELESSNESS AND ACCESS TO SERVICES

1/ Have integrated strategies been put in place to tackle the multiple disadvantages and changing needs of homeless people in line with the active inclusion approach, i.e. with coordinated strategies covering cash benefits, personalized employment pathways and support from social and health services?

2/ Please describe the policies that homeless people can benefit from in the following domains:

-Employment (e.g. training, job counselling, supported employment and wage subsidies)  

- Health and care (coverage of homeless people)

- Social assistance (e.g. day centres and street work for those who are sleeping rough)

Employment

In general, it can be claimed that homeless people are also present in the target groups of programmes that aim at the labour market integration of disadvantaged people.

At the same time, some employment promotion programmes have also been launched where the target groups are exclusively made up of homeless people. First, in 2000 the Labour Market Fund financed the promotion of the employment of 300 homeless people: use of active job-seeking services, social work needed for the entrance to the labour market, mental-hygienic developments, supply of equipment needed for work (travel expenses, clothes, working clothes, food, etc.). In 2007–2008, these elements of support were completed with the take-over of some of the taxes and contributions being payable by the employers on wages.

In addition to national resources, EQUAL also supported the employment of homeless people. About current programmes with EU funding see the section “Larger support programmes – homelessness”.

Health and care

Under the Hungarian legal regulations, for people being homeless within the meaning of Act III of 1993 on social administration and social services the state of Hungary covers the insurance if they are not insured to have healthcare services. Accordingly, homeless people may become eligible for healthcare services, as well. (However, the enforcement of these legal regulations is not always free of problems.)

Towards the provision of proper healthcare service to homeless people, the National Health Insurance Fund (OEP) finances family doctor services for homeless people: 1 in each region and 8 in Budapest; for the benefit of those who have no physicians in ordinary in the lack of proper places of residence. Concurrently, in recent years OEP has financed the establishment of the so-called homeless healthcare centres that – in addition to general healthcare services – convalescent capacities are also operated for proper follow-up healthcare.

Other social assistance

Daytime services

Established for homeless people, daytime facilities are available for the cleaning of clothes, washing, community programmes or in most cases meals, too. Furthermore, a number of daytime institutions offer counselling and assistance in the handling of official matters, searching jobs.

On the national level, in 2008 the daily average of people visiting the 93 daytime care centres was 6,300. In Budapest, the corresponding attendance of the 31 daytime care centres was 3,100 people.

Street social work

The purpose of street social work is the identification of people living rough, the provision of services to them, the encouragement of the use of institutional services. Street care services tend to coordinate their work with the competent social, healthcare, etc. organizations, service providers, as well as the dispatcher services acting in the given region.

In 2008, 85 street services were operated, out of which 32 in Budapest.

Regional dispatcher services

The dispatcher services operating in the 7 regions of Hungary:  

  • receive  reports in relation to people living rough and being in critical situations,
  • in the light of the given reports, they notify the competent street social worker service, or initiate the provision of any other services as needed,
  • keep daily contacts with the street social worker service providers, institutions, healthcare duty services and other service providers providing services to the target group,
  • collect and systemize the capacity data as well as information connected with the mode of use of the institutions operating in the region for homeless people and on other services associated with the homeless people,
  • maintain a computerized database for the actions taken in relation to the reports,
  • inform the regional methodological institute of their activities and the problems detected.

3/ Are there any national standards on the quality of social services for homeless people?  What are the procedures for ensuring their implementation and execution?  

As it has been mentioned above, still today the guaranteed, central state financing and regulations have a key role in social services. The condition of supports disbursed normatively is that the given services should have a proper operating permit. Operating permits may be granted only to service providers that are compliant with the Ministerial Decree prescribing the quality requirements of social services. These provisions cover the infrastructure requirements of the individual services, institutions (e.g. square meters for living, washrooms, toilets etc. per service user), the minimum professional headcount to be engaged, professional qualifications for the individual jobs, substances of services, minimum documents, rights of the clients, establishment of payable fees, procedure of lodging appeals, etc.

At the present, these requirements are mostly static, function as inputs, but carry little information in relation to the quality and efficiency of professional activities, achievements.

The proper observance of the requirements related to the service standards is controlled by the maintaining organization each year, i.e. the appointed organization of the Social and Guardianship Office, as well as the local notaries and other authorized organizations.

MONITORING AND EVALUATION

1/ Is there a national or regional regular reporting system/ mechanism in place?  Specify whether strategies to improve data collection have been put in place.

There are several systems operated in the field of data collection.

  • The most important one is the data collection round performed by the Hungarian Statistical Office to gather the fundamental data of the service system, such as number of institutions, service providers, capacity figures, types of maintainers, headcount data of professional employees, number of persons attended and their most important characteristics, volumes of the expenditures on the individual services. These data, however, are summarized only with 18 months of delays in general, and therefore they can be used for the planning of the budget or programmes just to a very limited extent.
  • Towards the provision of up-to-date information, 2006 saw the launching of the register of social services, containing the fundamental data of services and maintainers with operating permits (types of services, addresses, number of sites, capacities, number of persons attended, number of new persons admitted since the previous report, number of persons leaving the services, etc.). Today, this system still needs to be improved, and for this purpose EU resources are also available.
  • In addition to the foregoing, data are also collected by the Hungarian State Treasury involved in the financing of the institutions in relation to the utilization of the budgetary resources employed.

For the time being, the key challenge is posed by the linking of these sources of information to forge a unified database. There have been efforts made to this end, yet such a move would call for significant transformations.

The so-called homeless dispatcher services are operated on the regional level. The purpose of these services is to organize the provision of services to homeless people living rough or having found themselves in crisis situations in the streets. Towards this end, the dispatcher services perform daily telephone-based inquiries to record the utilization rates of night shelters, and there they have data in relation to the capacities that can be employed immediately. They have weekly data in relation to the utilization rates of temporary accommodation units. Relying on the regional databases, they are able to provide any interested party with information on the operation of the homeless service system (capacities, services, access, contacts). These dispatcher services receive the reports of the population, police, ambulance and other organizations in relation to homeless people, and organize their access to appropriate services. For the reception of reports, dispatcher services maintain 7/24 telephone lines. Dispatchers on duty are trained social experts.

In addition to the above, nation-wide databases there are special data collected by the individual organizations, mainly in relation to the users of the services of these organizations, as well as their services.

2/ Please present any evaluation, and its outcome, carried out in your Member States of policies addressed to homelessness and housing exclusion.

In 2006, the State Audit Office published its report entitled the “Control of the institutional system for homeless people”, wherein the following key findings are made:

  • Around the change of regime the structuring of the service system was in fact started from the roots. As a consequence of the demonstrations of homeless people, a still unmatched professional alliance was established with the cooperation of various organizations, service providers, non-governmental organizations, churches, governmental actors, and basically it was not initiated by the governmental actors.
  • Since their effective dates, the Social Act and the Ministerial Decree regulating the enforcement of the Act have been modified on several occasions, yet the tasks of the provision of service to homeless people are not aligned with the needs and services actually rendered. It has not been clarified what belongs to the tasks of the provision of service to homeless people, the ways out of the state of homelessness have not been described. The modifications of the relevant legal regulations are not coherent, and sometimes contradictory.
  • The definition of the budget frames pertaining to the operation and development of services has not been preceded by fundamental calculations. In the field of the provision of services to homeless people, no planned and actual national data are available in relation to the types of services, revenues of services, costs of operations.  In this respect, governmental and non-governmental maintainers do not provide clearly specified information of standardized contents.
  • Within the organization of the Ministry of Social Affairs, however, in 2002 a ministerial commissioner was appointed to handle the problems of the administration of homeless people efficiently with respect to the social significance of services to homeless people. In the examined years, background materials and governmental proposals were made in relation to homelessness wherein objectives were set, and in 2004 a governmental resolution set forth the tasks that may serve as the basis of a strategy to be elaborated in the future, also with respect to the results of the associated pilot experiments.
  • In the lack of sufficient properties, the local governments have only means of social policy to provide services to homeless people. The current financing system in itself does not mean a motivation for local governments for the prevention of people becoming homeless, and assisting in leaving the state of homelessness.  
  • In Budapest, towards the improvement of the efficiency of their services organizations involved in services to homeless people have collected information in relation to homeless people in Budapest, and coordinated the activities of the organizations involved in the provision of services. As a result of coordinated tasks, services to homeless people in Budapest have improved.
  • In order to have coordinated and more efficient provision of services and operations for homeless people, cities in the countryside also typically see the cooperation of organizations involved in services to homeless people and other service providers.
  • The effective legal regulations ensure that homeless people can as well have healthcare services. Caring for homeless people in need of nursing belongs to both healthcare services and social services, and therefore – due to this “cross-border” situation – calls for coordinated assistance from both areas. In the light of the urgent service demands arising in relation to the provision of healthcare services, in parallel with the healthcare system specific healthcare services have been established within the social service system as well.

Registration (“Headcount”)

After 2005, 2008 witnessed a new round of registration of people living roofless in Budapest. This registration was designed to make an attempt to determine the number of people living rough at the given time. The key findings of the survey were:

  • at the night from 02 to 03 February 2008, in Budapest at least 700 and at a maximum 1,400 were sleeping at visible locations without shelter,
  • approximately the same number of people sleeping at hidden locations make use of the services for homeless people,
  • 2,000 other people were spending the night at shelters,
  • 3,000 other people were living in temporary (or family) accommodations for the homeless, and thus

a total number of 7–8,000 homeless people could be found in Budapest.

“Survey of 03 February”

Since 1999, the Work Team of the Third of February has been conducting a questionnaire survey on 03 February each year among homeless people living in temporary accommodations, hostels or being in contact with street follow-up care services. The survey that used to be made only in Budapest has spread to the other cities of the country in recent years. The questionnaire data collection is voluntary, and therefore not all the organizations and homeless people participate. The questionnaire data collection embraces only those people who are in contact with hostels or street services. On 03 February 2009, a total number of 7,121 homeless people, with 2,862 roofless among them, responded to the questions. The main findings of the work team have been on the basis of the obtained data:

  • Most of the people without shelter live in the countryside (Bp.: 1,360, countryside: 1,502).
  • In Budapest, more homeless people live in hostels/certain types of accommodation (Bp.: 2,326, countryside: 1,933).
  • In certain county seats, the numbers and proportions of homeless people and those with no shelters are outstandingly large
  • In the majority of the settlements, the situation has not changed considerably in the past one year, but in several settlements – Miskolc, Székesfehérvár, Szolnok – the number of people living in public areas have grown significantly.
