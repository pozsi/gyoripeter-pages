1995-Hajléktalanság 1990-1995 FEANTSA ANGOLUL

PÉTER GYÔRI

Homelessness - and its implications in Hungary

1990 - 1995

Brief report

1995

Summary

I have compiled the following short, summarizing study for the Third International Congress of FEANTSA titled ‘Where to Sleep Tonight; Where to Live Tomorrow?’ in order that the delegates of the Congress get an insight into the current state of homelessness in Hungary. In my report I endeavour to present who are considered homeless in Hungary, who live under this threat, how high the number of these people is and under what circumstances they actually live. In the second part of my report I would like to inform you briefly about the most important milestones in the management of homelessness in Hungary in the past and the concrete changes occurred in the legislation, finance and social service system catering for the homeless.

1. Who are the homeless?

It might sound morbid but it can even be subject to linguistic-sociological research how ‘homelessness’ is defined by whom, where and when. However, all of us know both from our everyday practice and the literature of the profession how important it is to give an accurate definition for this hardly describable state of existence. Briefly, I would summarize you some of these definition types.

(Everyday definition) According to my experiences there are several, parallel and continuously changing definitions about the homeless in the public’s mind, which views are based upon the everyday visual experiences, the images transmitted by the media and the evaluation of their own situation of these people. Hence, those are seen as homeless who do not work, become despondent, are filthy, stinking, are drifting aimlessly, become an alcoholic, sleep in the streets, spend the day wandering around in railway stations, are perceived as tramps, hooligans and beggars. Others believe that the homeless are actually ‘poor people’ who do not have an apartment for some reasons, are marginalised, lost support, are seriously ill and do not receive enough help to be able to cope with their problems on their own.

(Sociological definition) It is based on the most important features of this state of existence which clearly distinguish it from other groups of people, saying for example that ‘people who are unable to arrange housing for themselves are called homeless’. Due to the problems of statistical registration definitions often become limited and only those are considered homeless who seek assistance, i.e. show up at organisations established specifically for supporting this group. Not only for the reason of understanding the direct social situation but the social risks and processes as well, it is a logical classification accepted also by the profession to distinguish between the ‘effectively homeless’, those who do not have any housing, and the ’potential homeless’, those who live in the danger of homelessness.

(Definition of the aid organisations) All the aid organisations use their own definitions which are either more comprehensive and general or rather specific, always depending on their operations. They consider those people to be homeless - and hence a client - who turn to them for help in the welfare shelters, the free refectories, the heated public rest rooms, get registered by social workers of the street, are helpless, starving, have to go without a home, had to flee away, belong to no country or were hit by a natural catastrophe.

(Bureaucratic, legal definition) Such a definition either does or does not exist in a given country (or settlement) at a certain time. In case of compiling a policy on and taking measures in homelessness affairs it becomes unavoidable to define this term due to other necessary legal descriptions such as the subject of the procedure, the responsibilities, authorities and financial arrangements. (We will elaborate this in more details later).

Due to all these it is extremely difficult to give an internationally acceptable definition of the homeless - for the benefit of international comparison of figures. A good example of this is the jointly prepared working definition laid down in the document titled ‘First Situation Analysis of Homelessness in the European Community’s Member States (1992)’ which says, ‘he who has not got or lost his habitation facility and is unable to change this situation without social-charity assistance is a homeless.’

1.2.        Their number and composition

(Statistical estimate) Due to the lack of intentional statistics one can rely only on the results of the census which, however, just fails to reach the majority of the actual (‘effectively’) homeless. On the other hand the potential homeless, that is those living in the danger of homelessness, can be relatively reliably estimated. Their number reaches some 200-220,000 (2 percent of the population) in Hungary today. Assessing the probability of becoming effectively homeless it can only be supposed that the number of the effectively homeless is some 20-30,000 people whose 50 percent live in Budapest.

(Estimate based on client turnover) The Shelter Foundation has been operating a fully open charity, administrative office for the homeless since 1990. The number of those appearing and getting registered there reach 20,000 people today. According to the surveys conducted among homeless people in the capital it is a public knowledge today that 50 percent of them have already visited this office. On the basis of this finding and taking the changes in the situation also into account the number of homeless people in Budapest is estimated to be some 10,000.

(Estimate based on bed turnover) On the basis of the total figures of the turnover data of all the operational social shelters of Hungary experts estimate the number of the effectively homeless (already did and did not visit overnight shelters) to be 20,000.

1.3.        Where do they come from?

According to everyday practical experiences the following main input groups exist:

(Young people breaking away from their families) dominantly with a problematic family background characterised by the alcoholism of the parents, their deviant lifestyle and crisis caused by their divorce.

(Young people on the verge of adulthood, released from children’s homes) who after becoming 18 years old automatically leave the children’s homes, the state care, and have no family background or habitation to return to.

(Young- and middle-aged drug-addicts) whose majority derive from the earlier groups. They are thrown out of their families, their relationships break up. They fail to find a job, their employment relations are generally unsettled anyway, and their lifestyles and companionship are often entangled with the mildest or the middle layers of the criminal underworlds.

(Alcoholics, psychologically or somatically ill and handicapped people) who are capable of providing themselves with the necessary daily food in-take due to some occasional work or in some other ways, whilst others are incapable of work or are even officially registered as disabled, living in poverty. Their relations are often broken, they have been expelled from their homes or left it behind voluntarily- if they had any at all -, and are now drifting aimlessly in the world.

(Old, decrepit tramps) who usually come from the previous groups. They have been living this stabilised tramp lifestyle for a long time which is special even within the homeless. They compromise with the institutionalised opportunities and lifestyles only as a last resort.

(People just released from prisons) who have lost their homes - if they had ever had any. Their care and rehabilitation is fully unaddressed. Some of them have actually no other choice but homelessness whilst others use this subculture as a hiding place.

(Occasional workers, ex-commuters, lodgers of workers’ hostels, unemployed) are usually middle-aged people capable of working, often even with qualifications but definitely not working in their trade. Their wages do not even cover the increasing rents, their income is uneven and they are often left without a job or money. They are hit both by the increasing regional unemployment and the increase in the costs of everyday existence, simultaneously. Wherever they look for work they generally fail to find a home, or have lost it and become homeless. Still, within this state they are the ones who endeavour to live as settled a life as possible.

(Divorced people) who after the collapse of their marriage or partnership are left without a home and failed to recover both financially, mentally and physically. They become completely displaced, get entangled in alcoholism and deviance, and finally find themselves in the state of homelessness.

(Evicted people) who did not pay the rent or the fees of public utilities, were using the flat illegally (as they had occupied it by force, been only sub-tenants or their care and inheritance contract with an old person failed). They are made homeless through an official procedure and their future is of no further interest to anybody. This often happens to single mothers with young children or to young couples and partners.

1.4.        Homelessness - full marginalisation

The state of homelessness causes radical negative changes in the individual’s physical, mental and social conditions, or is the result of such changes in his existing conditions. Not even the elementary needs of the homeless are met, they obtain food at the level of poverty, their clothing is minimal, and they are unable to satisfy their basic hygienic requirements. Thus, they run a much higher risk of getting ill than the average people, their health condition falls far behind any standard and their chances for receiving treatment and recovering are almost negligible.

Their social relations are very limited, they have no families or their family relations have broken up just like their employment and neighbourhood ones. At best they manage to build loose internal group-relations. They can hardly make use of the ‘large institutional systems’, they need external assistance in being able to get connected to these organisations (such as social security, municipalities, health care, employers).

Their constantly unstable conditions create a mental crisis both in their social relations and physical existence which is dominated by the state of ‘social isolation’. Their marginalised social situation can be improved by organisations and institutions catering for the homeless which, however, also have to cope with their own marginalised conditions.

2.        History of homelessness

2.1.        Past and recent years

According to the attitude of the era before 1945 ‘he who is unable to provide himself and his family with a home due to his circumstances is considered homeless’. In those times it was an underlying principle that in such cases the community had to cater for the housing of the homeless: such families were accommodated in emergency flats, and individuals in social shelters. Originally, families could stay in an emergency flat (or as it was called earlier, in ‘family rooms’) free of charge for a maximum period of two weeks which could be extended to three months.

According to a survey in 1936, 4,574 families lived in emergency flats and 3,517 ones in sub- and bed-tenancy in Budapest. ‘The authorities were not in the position to be able to provide depressed families evicted from sub- and bed-tenancies to the streets with emergency flats, thus, these families were forced to keep wandering from one sub-tenancy to the other until they could afford to cover more or less their rents from their occasional income and government and social allowances. Should this collapse, there was no other choice than to separate members of the family and arrange accommodation for the individuals at acquaintances, relatives or in overnight shelters.’

Before World War I six large shelters were set up with the support of the capital, in the organisation and management of the ‘Association of Shelters for the Homeless’. In the 1930s temporary and additional shelters were also established, and the police opened temporary heated public rest rooms for the homeless fleeing away from their huts in order to avoid freezing. The capital set up the Central Bureau of the Homeless in 1936 which kept uniform records of the homeless registering, sent people to shelters and granted financial and cloth aid. 4,534 people were registered in the Bureau in January 1938. In winter free day-time heated rest rooms were operated which everybody could enter and they even received a rather modest meal. Overnight they could have some sleep on wooden benches in the police’s heated public rest rooms. At the four railway stations of Budapest (and at 14 provincial stations) the Railway Mission was there to help. In the 1930s roughly 200 charity and voluntary associations worked in the capital. Several shelters, old people’s homes, children’s homes and similar institutions were operated.

After the reconstruction following World War II, namely after 1950, the political situation changed dramatically and the socialist system administered by the new state swept away the regulation, the institutions and organisations catering for the homeless (all that are mentioned above), though not the phenomenon itself. This situation existed until the late 1980s, early 1990s, when the so called political transition gained momentum.

2.2.        Events after 1990

Regulation

(Social Act) In 1993 the so called ‘Social Act’ came into force which - due to the undeniable existence of homelessness, the already established and operational aid organisations and the pressure of the lobby groups - deals with homelessness at a statutory level and codifies the basic elements of the Hungarian homelessness affairs.

The Act gives two different definitions on who is considered homeless and connects the two different legal positions with different public administration procedures.

a.        He who has not got a registered address of residence or whose registered address of residence is a shelter for the homeless is considered homeless. That government agency with authority in social affairs has the competence in arranging and executing social administration procedures for homeless people on whose territory of competence the homeless person concerned can be found. The board of representatives of the settlement’s municipality provides temporary aid for persons in emergency situation jeopardising their physical existence or suffering from temporary or permanent problems threatening their physical existence.

b.        He who spends the nights in public spaces or in other places of habitation not built for residence is considered homeless. Disregarding its authority and competence the settlement’s municipality is compelled to provide temporary aid for the needy and accommodation for the homeless if otherwise delay jeopardised the life and physical condition of the needy and homeless person. (The official body providing temporary aid and care informs the competent authority about its measures taken, immediately. At the same time it can apply for a reimbursement of its expenses incurred in providing temporary shelter.)

The Act also defines some aid organisations as well, which by that time have been operating for long, (and which are better detailed in other legal regulations), and prescribes the establishment of such organisations in certain settlements.

The day-time heated public rest rooms serve as shelters for the homeless people during the day. The overnight shelters and temporary hostels cater for the homeless’ rest in the night or for their temporary residence. These compulsory forms of care have to be set up before 31 December, 1997.

(Housing Act) The new Housing Act was prepared in parallel with the Social Act and was enforced early 1994.

Its most relevant parts in respect to our topic are as follows:

o        the abolition of the eligible’s application and waiting list system for housing

o        the abolition of the municipalities’ obligation to provide accommodation for the homeless,

o        the increased risk existing tenants run in having to terminate their tenancy (cancellation of tenancy, changes in the regulation of rents)

o        the decreased guarantees on tenancy (rights, increase in rents, contracts).

Concerning the assistance and protection systems the new legislation has proved to be rather incomplete due to the above changes in its regulations, whilst factors jeopardising housing and physical existence of people have been increasing dramatically.

These changes in the regulation have to be evaluated as a complex, and when seeing their joint consequences it can be stated that such significant changes in attitudes and concepts did occur which result in a transition from a quasi-welfare system to a crisis management one.

Finance

Just as the changes in the legislation, the modifications in the funding methods also prove how and to what extent the political and budgetary decision-makers accept the homelessness affairs as a ‘new challenge’. Its short history in the 1990s is as follows:

1989-90:        spontaneous initiatives of the homeless, (railway stations, public squares, public security, Rumanian refugees), crisis team, establishment and operation of the round table in the Ministry of Welfare, search for the solutions for the first time, fire-fighting, temporary and non-institutional reactions, temporary and occasional supply of shelters and funding

1990:        establishment of the Ministry of Welfare’s Crisis Management Bureau, the round table is replaced by a centralised, bureaucratic, command driven but institutional management of the issue, a tender system is set up by the Ministry for encouraging the establishment of new organisations and initiatives among which - beside others - organisations catering for the homeless are also allowed

1991:        special targeted tender opportunity both for the NGOs and the municipalities in case of crisis, targeted but occasional funding

1992:        regulation of the Ministry of Welfare on institutions catering for the homeless, though, it does not cover funding only the institutions’ eligibility for application

1993:        Social Act containing the rights of the homeless, the obligation of the municipalities to establish the necessary institutions, the opportunity of ‘application for institutional norm’ at the Ministry of Welfare which is a more stable but not fully accepted form of funding

1994:        the Parliament defines and grants norms controlled in the national budget for temporary shelters (free refectories, day-time heated rest rooms, etc.), stable but selective funding and the rest remains on occasional tendering

1995:        the Social Security invites a tender on the health care service of the homeless and provides finance in its contracts, regular usage of the round table on public welfare in the decision-making process

Features of the social assistance network

In a few years a significant social network has developed for the assistance of the homeless in Hungary - in spite of all the facts that the country is in an unfavourable economic situation, the effective value of social budgets keeps decreasing and the existing networks fail to meet the actual needs.

Similarly to others the Hungarian system catering for the homeless is also characterised by the fact that it primarily focuses on the capacity of the shelters and hostels, and within that on providing accommodation for single men. Homeless families have the possibility to try to arrange for habitation through the housing application procedures, with very modest chances. Within the system catering for the homeless the proportion and role of the NGOs are much larger than in any other fields of the social service, and within that they rather focus on dining and social assistance than accommodation.

At the same time it can also be stated that the system is comprised of solutions for the crisis and does not provide the solution of the crisis. It deals with primary residence and accommodation and not a breakout from this form of existence. It is also typical that whilst the system has major deficiencies the network catering for the homeless keeps changing, expanding and specialising.
