Budapest,1989.

Gábor, László - Gyõri, Péter:

DAY-TO-DAY CRISIS MANAGEMENT

(public utilities and non-paying consumers in a city)

This study aims at summing up the partial results of a wider research project. Concerned with state ownership, more precisely with the so-called "welfare council flats", our aim is to seek answers through politico-sociological methods legal history and empirical sociology on two questions: what has been the (so far untold) role of housing policy in the struggle of various social groups and forces to acquire materials goods since 1945, and in which fields are social struggles intensified by the present wide-spread economic and social crisis, revealing the total absence of truely effective social construction.

In this study the reader will find a few results of an empirical survey conducted by interview and question form methods among Budapest families who had been unable to pay electricity, gas, district heating and similar utility bills and/or rent for council flats for longer or shorter periods of time. Thus our analysis is concentrated on a relatively detailed description of the participants of some particular conflicts studied in housing menagement, the conflicting interests of public utilities and official (council) landlords vs. the municipal rent collecting company, the latter°s internal structure and functioning, the motivation of those who have to collect rents and charges, and a differentiated group description of non-paying customers (the latter also beking an attempt made at describing various groups of the city poor); in our view this is the only way of understanding in a sociologically complex manner where the lines of conflict really run between the different interest groups and subcultures, and what the social game called "non-paying rents and charges" are really about.

The actual weight carried by the problem examined here is indicated by the fact that of the approx. 800 thousand homes lived in by 2 million Budapest in habitants, 420 thousand are council flats, and of their tenants about every sixth (approx. 68 thousand families or households) was in some kinel of arrears at the beginning of 1989. The number of families in arrears has trebled over the past five yeats. A significant section of them fail to pay up "only" occasionally for a month at a time, but the propation of those unable to "catch up with themselves", accumulating several months of arrears, is gradually increasing. As a consequence, and in the wake of the "naturally" growing irritation of public ut~7ity companies and rent collectors, more and more legal proceedings have been initiated (at the moment, nearly seven thousand for failure to pay rent and about eight thousand for not settling utility bills) and many households have had their public utility supply cut off (weekly 150 on the average).

The public is just as much shocked by these obvious signs of sprending pauperism as the politicians and managers affected are. That type of social bankruptcy and collapse of nonns is part of the general insolvency of the economy, where a wilde range of companies and institutions owed a total of Ft. 140 million to the Gas Supply Company in the end of 1988 in Budapest alone (with another 250 m~lion Ft owed to the Company by residents): the Hungarian Electricity Trust could not collect some 1.5 thousand m~llion Ft. from industrial firms (and about a tenth of that sum from domestic ecusumers in the entire country). At present, "bankruptcy proceedings" can be more easely initiated against households that are in arrears of a few thousand Forints each than against state firms that have accumulated debts of measured in tens of millionsand openly admitting bankruptcy.

1. Collecting Company: Squeezed In Between Public Utility Companies and Consumers

On the face of it, two opposing interest groups clash within the frames of the ever growing number of non. payers and the ever stringent legal proceedings triggered off by them: consumers vs. suppliers, But the real situation is far more complicated than that; the combined party of "suppliers" is heavily entangled in contrasting interests and conflicts.

The consumers' and suppliers' sides are in contact through a fairly large group of people, the collectors.They represent the Public Utilities demanding their fees and the Landlord wishing to collect the rents. The actual act of paying becomes personal through the coilectors.

That type of personalization of contact between "Suppliers" and "Consumers" is a customary form, but not a regular one. Companies transfer money via bank accounts and so does a minority of fhe population. The latter only meet the colIectors when the meters are read if - in new system-built honsing developments where the meters are outside the flats - at all. The very work, and thus the personal character, of cdlectors is called into question by a general adoption of paying via bank order or through the post. No representative of the insurance companies, banks or the tax office ever calls on houses: paying rents and settling accounts are functions of adult citizens. Needless to say, meters will have to be read in situ in the future too (if not quite as frequently as now), partly because finding a technical solution for this is not in anyanés interest for the time being, and partly because the chance of exercisting some control through the in situ inspection of meters could only be relinquished when there is a deeper mutual trust between consumers and suppliers (and the technical solutions involved).

The changes in the methods of coIlecting rents offer a clear example of the ptoblems on hand: in the past, the landlord's janitor was at all times present, thus exercising a direct control over the tenants and collecting rents smoothly on time. When tenement houses ware nationalized, that set-up remained basically unchanged for many years, until (for reasons not to be disenssed here) the functions of janitors were carved up among several participants, rent collecting having been taken over by an army of collectars - far more impersonal than the good old janitors. Lately, solutions inspired by distrust of the tenants keep cropping up, suggesting that rents be collected, and the actual use of council flats be supervised, by representatives of the state housing management firms, who could more directly act on behalf of the state or the government as the landlord.

The personal character of paying cash not only keeps changing form and method, coming to the fore or receding slightty into the background, but the very person of the cash collector represents different things fot the individual households. For many people, a cash collector is merely a "door-to-door cashier", whose person is utterly unimportant and interchangeable. Others rely on the personality of collector, who is familiar with the neighbourhood, is conversaht with the comings and goings of his or her "clientele", i.e. when they can be found at home, in which part of the month they have got enough money etc., and can cone to an accommodation to the clients - at least withing reasonable limits. But the collector can only become a 'man of ttust' only for those who, even if momentarily, share their problems with him, asking him to come back later or requesting a bit of patience and understanding (since cash or rent can be collected patiently or impatiently, with some elbow room allowed in certain permissive and retaliating procedures of the collecting business).

Rent collecting is a unique profession, for - owing to the inaqual relations inherent in it - collectors represent "authority", even if they happen to be understanding and

tlexible, or - nota bene - "appeasable". Although provided with some real tools of authority (establishing accurate date of payment, when consumers should be at home, payment summons, initiating court proceedings, putting lead seals on meters etc.), collectors are seldom compelled to use them, their funetion merely consists of collecting due rents and charges, in other words acting as the "beggars of the authority", with as much decency as possible. Although these features have not much to do woth the standard of service, these are the aspects that can be best kept in evidence - and at a lower risk.

The efficiency of collectors depends on the quantities of bills collected and the time spent on the job. Through careful organization, the time needet to collect money can he minimized, but for that schedules must be fully adapted to the consumers' needs. Consequently, a good collector must know hís or her distríct thorughly and should of course collect there for a long time; moreover, some means of maintaining "discipline" among the consumers can also come handy. The collector hos to be familiar with the time schedules of all the inhabitants in the district, i.e. who can be found in this or that part of the day, or solely on rest days etc.

This means that the advantage offered to the collector of having no set working hours is reduced to a minimum as soon as a collector wants to organize his or her work schedule for maximum efficiency.

But within certain limits, collectors are fairly free to economize with their time and energy, establishing their own order of importance for official and private matters Thus taking care of small children or sick and elderly relatives at home can be harmonized with rent collecting, along with cooking dinner for the family, tending household farms, altending daytime educational , doing cutwork for 6rms and other such similar engagements. In comparison with other jobs of a somewhat similar nature, collecting is a less closely defined and responsible a job than that of a postman, but is definitely more complicated than that of the newspaper delivery man.

As is the case of wages earned by postmen, papermen and in general the employees in different services, collectors are typically given low wages, tipping being reikened on. The size and structure of tipping, however, cannot be calculated and depends on a host of factors, Such as people in heavily tipped professions tending to give tips more regularly than those with fixed incomes who, in turn, would be ashamed of failing to tip the obviously underpaid collectors; furthermore, being among the potential delinquents, low-income consumers are deeply interested in "buying" the goodw~lI of rent collectors. As observed on many occasions, consumers who have accumulated the highest arrears tend to give the handsomest tips - to an amount that often borders on corruption.

Nevertheless, even without knowing enough of the details it can be stated that the custom of tipping diminishes the sxstem of financial incentives established by the collection company (which is fairly inefficient anyhow). Offering an extremely low extra income, the system is less and less competitive with tipping. And what makes things worse, the collection company, while turning a blind eye to tipping, partly to eliminate the risk of bribery and partly because collecting has not been acknowledged as a "tipped" job, does take the fact into consideration by deliberately issuing "advantageous" and "disadvantageous" districts to its collectors and trying to draw these up in such a way as to achieve a more or less just arrangement for everyhody, lest tension should inerease within the ranks to a point where nobody would apply for the job.

Several other internal conflicts and contradictions within collecting as a job are also the result of low wages. Over the past decades, the job has been taken over by wanen; not only "housewives" are ready to take such jobs but also university students needing some

extra income or old-age pensioners unable to make ends meet etc. It is not surpusing that with such staff it is extremely difficult to produce a high degree of efficiency, especially in the so-called disadvantageous or 'hard~ areas. On the other hand, with the standard of living gradually sinking all over the country, the entire system of collecting becomes "soft" on payments experiencing the same economic hardships in their private lives, the collectors are unable to believe any longer in the justness of rents and chorges, the legitimation of chargmg such chorges is at stake and the excuse of "through no fault of one' s own" for failing to pay is quite credible for the collectors themselves.

Collectors have a close, although not a day-t-day relationship with their bosses. They take their packets of bills for the month, collect. the money, book the items, send the money to the Company by post, and finally visit the branch office again to renderin account, after which the entire process is begins allover again for the forthcoming month. Apart from the incentives offered by the Company, all that could as well as be a one-man undertaking for every collector, but actually is normal employment subject to the traditional bureaucratic expectations and pressures.

Established in 1948 and unique in its kind anywhere in the world, the Collecting Company is not really interested in collecting arrears, its income being proportional to the number of meters to be read off and the number of flats to be visited. Although yielding no profit, any action taken beyond those basic tasks is none the less a contractual liab~ity. With anybody unable or unw~7ling to pay, collectors are actually powerless. Application of pressure is limited by regulations of social policy and certain political considerations. Thus it is in the rules of the Company to refrain from cutting off ut~lities to delinquents before double holidays, the prolonged absence or hospitalization of consumers. At present, the real driving force behind the Company's actions is not su much the profit netted, but rather a peculiar ethos of meeting the requirements and discharging the functions set for the Company, some internal "must" of coming up to the expectations of the bureacratic machinery, working according to its own rules and establishing its own norms. Consequently, however for rational and understandable the reasons for delinquency may be in certain cases, failing to pay is regarded by the entire system as "intolerable" and is to be sdved by the collector or his or her branch office.

Another essential dciving force strongly affecting delinquents is the collectors' effert to cany ont their work as quickly as possible, this being also in the interest of the Company. More visits would mean more collectors and vice versa and any consumer in arrears would inevitably increase the number of visits and cause complications to the work schedules. Collectora and the Company alike deeply dislike delinquents, their "persuansion" to pay up at last being "dirty work", involving extra administration. It seems that the "softpedalling" of certain collectors and the periodically "liberal" attitude of the Company towards delinquents are due to lack of capacity and not for kumaere considerations.

The barnch offices themselves, although at the price of much paper work, can adopt the tactics of passing the problem of delinquency to the higher echelons of the Company; this they cem do by transferring a properly prepared documentatiofi of the consumers in arrears in their districts to the legal department of the Company, they have met expectations and vid themselves of a burden, at least temporarily. Moreover, the missing sums do not affect adversely their work efficiency indices, since they are in the in-trays of the Legal Department.

The interst of the L,egal Department to settle outstanding accounts as quickly as possible coincides with the Fu~al objectives of the Company. Provided that the schedules of the court permit it, the Legal Department Passes on the documents for them to be

executed. It is characteristic of the lack of co-operation and joint interest that quite a number of documents travel all the way from the execution office of the court to the Legal Department, branch office and the collector responsible for the consumer in question on account of some real or fake deficiency in the data. Such a passing back of the buck of course entails extra work and gives rise to some kind of disciplinary actions.

The overall motivation of the Company to make delinquents pay up is also based on such contradictions. On the one hand, it is one of the primary functions of the Company to collect rents and charges, making full use of its tactical, work organization, administrative, law enforcing etc. means; on the other the economic and other interests of the Company are much more differentiated than that.

Along with some other historical reasons, the basic idea of establishing the Company was to render the conecting of several utility charges more economical in the capital through a single, unified me agency, reading off the meters and presenting the bills at a siningle time in every household, rather than doing all this separately. Thus the Company is interested right up to this very day in engaging as many customers as possible for its fully established system, data stock, data processing network etc. (This is how chimney sweeping charges, rents, home insurance, certain advertising fees ete. have been gradually added to electricity and gas bills.) Thus the internal motivation of the Company associated with collecting rents and charges is basically expansive.

No wonder that "fiddling" with delinquents cleerly disturbs the fundamentally expansive attitude of the Company. The public utilities, as customers, do not like the extra expenses involved in collecting arrears, and expect the collecting firm be more effective, and without any additional charges. As a consequence, incentives and motivation remain limited at the company level. It is interesting to see how "business" discussions with the customers are interwoven with expectations appealing to certain traditions, pressures arising ftom authoritative positions and the mobilization of "Godfathers", as well as the all-important personal contacts of the managers. As a result, the Company's negotiation positions can be highly varied with different customers and public utility firms: the partners enjoing monopolisdc positions in their own fields try to hold each other at bay, and the range includes high-handed and peremptory customers just as much as polite and well-behared ones. No wonder that profits and motivations vary from one branch of business to the other, although most of the differences tend to be lost in the internal mills of the company structure.

So far, the Company has been presented as an independent economic undertaking of a fundamentally bureaucratic character, since in spite of all changes in the company structure and external dependency (the originally municipal collecting "office" has become an independent firm), from the population' s point of view and as regards its funetioning in connection with collecting it has remained a "bureau". On the other hand, it should be bome in mind that over the past few years, the Company has dynamically developed an important computer branch, thus having become from an irctemal accountant of the Municipal Council a computer buse for the capital and also a commercial $rm on the computer service market; the surplus capacity of the computer system received for the modernization of its collecting function ean be "sold" on the marÍcet. It is characteristic of the company structure that the collecting branch and the computer centre are independent economic units within the overall framework in contractual relationship with each other; moreover, the "quasi market-oriented" computer branch is nearly as wightyas the collecting business; it is also worth mentioning that the young and highly trained managers have now more and more say in running the Company as a profitable undertaking, the general manager himself being a computer specialist.

At finalty, it should be mentioned that die Company's collecting activity covers a significant section of non-demestic consumers, amarg when meeting obligations promptly and accurately has been problematic for years and the present-day state of affairs is especially disheartening. Various industrial, agricultural, commercial frms, as well as governmental and political bodies etc. have often disregarded the Company's collectors (having been able to afford that attitude, being authoritative "bureaus" and "officies" themselve), and problems associated with liquidity crop up more often than ever. Hell is breaking loose" in the economy and the collectors can do very little against delinquent firms and offices (since they cannot be cut off from the mains, only be sued - a highly prolonged process). The dynamism and magnitude of the delingquency of economic bodies and official organizations are just as grave as those of the population.

The players in the game existing under the conditions of monopolies and a bureaucratic market, i.e. public utilities and other customers, the Company itself with its independent branches, the collectors and finally the consumers, are kept in a continuous state of turbulence by a system of a curious cooperation interwoven with traditions, customs, authoritative and administrative pressures, mutual double-crossing, sentiments, ideologies and even rational arguments. As con be seen from the above, the conflicts occur among participants who have unclear interests, roles and functions, the roles themselves being full of inherent conflicts.

~th the fast growth of the capitai and the infIux of large numbers of peopIe around the turn of the century, the market-oriented private undertakings gave evay to large public utilitiy firms. It is now inextricable what were the roles of a relatively undeveloped market, an insufficiently differentiated credit system, lack of capital on the venture market, the relatively low buying power of industrial and domestic consumers alike, a shift of power from entrepreneurs towards the bureacratic institutions, the idea of the modern city, the responsibility for public services in general tahen increusingly serviously by the municipal authorities and the technological characteristics of the highly capital-intensive utility networks.

It is clear that having left the pure market sphere of the entreprenurs, the public utility works started representing interests and funetions that were absolutely alien to the profit motive. In order to be able to meet public utility functions, the works took their shares of the taxes levied by the state and the city, and sone of their assets (partly company, partly municipal and paxtly state possessions) have been allotted to them as funding capital. In return, they are expected to supply the community, ensuring the normal funetioning of municipal institutions (plants, officies, households).

Also the consumers and city institutions taxed in one form or another expect their share of the city services to be taken for granted. Serices and utilities usually spread in cities graduatly (private and common wells appear first in the centre, being developed from there towards the outskirts; common wells are replaced by water supply mains first in multistorey houses, then also in the single-storey ones; open sewage ditches are first covered, then converted into an underground sewage disposal system; garbage disposal becomes an organized servíce; city gas illuminatíon of streets ís gradually replaced by electric power; electric supply becomes general in households; domestic gas supply is developed, first for cooking, then for heating; block and district heating systems are estaölished etc.). The very existence of city services and utilities is regarded by city dwellers as fact of cultural and civilized life, even if it may only be still scanty and limited, dividing the urban areas from the underdeveloped outskirts.

The urban civilization that has taken shape over a long period of history involves simple facts such as garbage no longer being dumped in the street in an "European city", sewage in not spilled outside one's gate, homes are provided with heating and lighting, the number of the rootless is limited etc. The type of coexistence known as urban civilization has developed many means and methods to prevent and avoid the sight of unpleasant situations (and equally as many for discriminating and hiding them). Since such anomalies would be in strong contrast with the well-established ordence for civilized life, their very sight is highly "embarrasing" to all.

Arrears in rents and charges are occasional or downright necessary projections of certain characteristic social situations or modes of existence. When searching for the causes, some characteristically different ways of functioning and types of organization are encountered, bespeaking the presence of small groups leading different ways of life even within small-size samples.

These characteristics of these ways of life cannot be established along one or two distinct factor but should be considered together and simultaneously; thus, the existence and non-existence of households, their manner of organization, stability and instability of family structures, social deviance, trends of consumption, forms of economy, functioning of institutions, characteristics of human relations, standards of living, state of technical development, characteristics of acquiririg a home, the quality of homes, the present state of affairs between the pasi and the future, the aspirations etc. must bee seen taken togethe r.

Needless to say, the delinquents included into our sample are mere representatives or indicators of individual way of life groups of unknown size, and we can take it as certain that the representatives of other groups of "delingquency" would only appear in a larger sample (e.g. the happy-go-lucky types who simply could or would not bother to pay bills regularly, highly skilled technicians extremly clever at offering plausible sounding explanations for messing payments, conscientous refusers, people who can seldom be found at home etc.).

The Rootless

About a half of the households visited belong to the "Rootless" category.

People have been met whose life has (finally?) been derailed or has never been on a track. In spite of the fact that their entire lives are geared towards finding a foothold scmewhere in life, they have not been able to achieve any stability so far. They could be called down and outs wrecks or pariahs, were they were than passive sufferers of their bot but also active participants, shaping their lives by characteristic means in a characteristic medium. It is just to call them the outcasts of society, marginalized people, sub-social elements, unintegrated individuals. But these descriptius rather indicate the social mechanisms that establish social standing and position, or more precisely the conflicts, of such people, and are less suitable for clearly defming their situations and everyday forms of living. It would be perhaps misleading to mark them, as a group, as poverty-stricken, paupers or even deviants, because these words denote more and less than the reality of the group tested.

The absence of any stability at all, based on howevera neagre financial basis, is one of the characteristics that separate the rootless from the "proletariat".

Under a certain standard of neediness, even the hope of ever attaining a relative stability is lost and losing one' s bearing in life gradually becomes irreversible. Necessity knows no law, as the old sow runs. In "homes" ruled by cold and hunger, concealed

prostitution soon offers itself as a viable alternative to penury, "well-doers" and "chums" of the same social position appear as overninght guests, irrespective of sex and age, and channels open to the "blackest market". At a certain stage, the very existence of such people cannot be maintained from one day to another without shoplifting, shady transactions, hustling and cheating. Although we do not have to accept that way of life, its presence and function must be understood (branding the majority of the group as mere "criminals" would be a gross overstatement).

It would be a mistake to believe that rootlessness also means a lack of economy in life. But in fact, that economy is realized in peculiar forms, among them - and in view of the frequency of its occurrence it should be given a special emphasis - the so-called 'partnership in life' or matrimony.

That phenomenon has many factors, one of them being that in the wake of a divorce or separation, one of the partners almost inevitably lands among the downcasts, having nothing more than the shirt on his buck. What usually follows is a servies of mores from one workers' hostel to another, and perhaps to cheap furnished rooms or even to being put-up as a favour. In those spheres of society, "shacking up" with somebody is one of the cheapest sdutions and has found wide-spread acceptance. Needless to say, "cheapness" is not the only aspect of the practice.

Rootlessness often goes hand in hand with loneliness, detachment from institutions and institutionalized norms in life, and even from the world of "normal" people striving after their own objectives. That predicament can be resolved by establishing a partnership with somebody.

Also some quite prosaic reasons can be found behind such solutions: underpants need washing, cooked food is also among our basic necessities, and some kind of a shelter where one can "go home" in the evening for a sound sleep is a must for all of us.

The absence of a regular income, the reasons for which will be dealt with later, also impels towards the painful necessity of finding a mate, as soon as possible. The method is not only about making use of "naturally" available services within an existing household or quasi-hosehold to bridging over the mariless state and/or escaping from an actually very expensive state of having no household at all, but also about the simple fact that a single income, if there is any at all, is usually insufficient to make ends meet.

Such relationships based on necessity are of course highly instable in themselves, partly due to the miserable circumstances and parfly as a consequence of a constant state of human, mental imbalance. Although any subsequent falilure of human relations inevitably leads to another crisis, that risk is inherent ín the "deal" (it is of course of great weight when a crisis ppcirs. amd wjocj pf tje éartmers is beinghit harder in it).

The particulars mentioned so far offer some explanation on the fact why middle-aged mothers with children share their lives with young men in their twenties more often than in other groups, or young mothers seek asylum with relatively well-to-do single men in their fifties ete.

The instability and frequency of soch relationships, the frequent changing of partners, determine the quality of the households. Obviously, there can hardly ever be sufficient time to establish some kind of regular and planned economy, based on stable financing. A normal, reasonable allocation of funds is never to be found even if there happens to be one regular wage-earner, or even two. Although this would not mean that money just "flows away" or is "thrown out of the window" in such households, at least not necessarily, but any allocation made is done in kind rather than by "finance".

Similarly to our Group 2 ("proletarian with low fixed incomes"), about every other household practices weekly or monthly shopping, even if at a lower level. This means that a (fairly significant) part of the income (pay, wages from odd jobs, pensions) is at once spent on basic food-stuffs (flour, lard, sugar, pastry, salt, coffee etc.). Continuously needed and non-perishable, those foodstuffs are regularly consumed until the next major shopping: thus by purchasing such fundamentals as soon as some money has Elowed in serves to ensure the conditions for elementary subsistence. Such traditional hoarding recalls war economy; and not suprisingly since these households are in a constant state of crisis, so to say, and a risk of household collapse demands a specific state of altert. By spending a part of the income on stockpiling without delay, the absolutely basic necessities can be provided for everybody in the household until the next income is received, since in case of emergency a few days can be lasted out on flour-cakes, vegetable stock, empty dumplings and similar crude foodstuff.

The money left after major shopping, thorugh according to several responders the entire income, is so little that there is nothing that could be ,~pread out". The only dif ferance among households has been observed only in whether rents and utility bills were included or set aside as fixed expenses (this being fairly uncharacteristic of those quarters, since household budgets would collapse from one "payday" to another anyhowj, or spending going on as necessary or possible, either leaving enough for rents or utility bills or not.

It is an essential characteristic of this type of household economy, and of the entire way of live, that the existencial imbalance and rootlessness causing loneliness go hand in hand with an absence of certain ossential human and economic relations ( with relatives, work-mates, neighbours etc.) which could offer some useful help in surmorinting the actual crisis. In spite of the fact that the households "run out" of money fairly often, and to quite significant extents at that, regular borrowing could not be observe (perhaps because nobody would lend them any money), and private debts rarely run high (because of the distrust caused by the lack of potential coverage). Owing to the "hard" barriers of the surroundings, debts tend to accumulate with the Collecting Company.

In the absence of the appropriate relations to act as a sound background, yet another motivation is apparent for striking up some kind of a cohabitation or concubinage - the sooner, the better.

A further factor, or group of factors, affecting that alert but rootless way of living is not being able to have wage-earning chances in the second economy. As a consequence, total earnings are even lower than those of others, thus blocking the way out of the hopeless mess and s life is not centered on work or job. Standing on only one leg is a serious risk in the work sphere, this being only compensatable by standing on more than one leg outside that sphere, if feasible.

A reason for failing to earn any money in the second economy is the insufficiency of informal relations that could help one to get second or third jobs, and also that fajlure can be lead back to imbalance and lack of integration of the derailed persons (among whom passive figures sticking to a single job throughout their lives can be found just as often as the socalled 'migrating birds' changing their jobs every now the and). But a limited ability to work is even more characteristic of them.

As a consequence of their entire careers, living conditions and, last but not least, adverse working conditions, many of the people concerned have serious health problems. Chronic alcoholism can of course be listed here as well, along with untreated and maltreated organic, mental and nervous diseases. It is a thought-provoking observation that a

serious malady or accident can trigger off some very fast procedures tovards losing one's footing; moreover, it seems as if rootless people are hit by accidents and certain types of maladies more often than others.

A finally, it should be made clear that by "roodess" people is meant not individuals possessing certain qualities or characteristics nor some state of affairs in which certain people tend to exist sooner or later (although at a certain level of society all these are inherent in the word); for the purposes of this study, the word denotes a clear-cut social group. The rootless people visited by us belonged to a more or less top region of the world of city dwellers having lost their foothold, for after all they Iived in flats instead of ' social homes ', "health institutions", chronic wards, prisons, youth hostels with regularly cut-off ties to the outer world, nor did they live alms-houses, under bridges or in obscure comers bearing no resemblonce to a human abode.

The other group examined beside the rootless city dwellers were the proletarians. Holding tow-paid white-collar, physical or auxiliary jobs, having nothing better than council flats allotted to them and leading possessing petty yet organized existences their incomes, standards of living and modes of living were essentiully set by their powerless social standing.

That section of our sample is further divided into theree; the denominations given each reflect a specific attitude: "vegetating at the surface", "manoeuvring at the surface" and "taking off from the surface". In relation to them, the rootless people are denoted as "whirling under the surface". In our case, the "surface" means the lowest level of membership in society, a state of integration in the world of normalIy Iiving people. Proletarian on Low Fixed Incomes

Thais category includes people whose life is "hard", but still "stay at the surface", sometimes only just", or are regarded as "endangered" by falling down the social ladder, but mostly living at the "minimum standard". typical of them is an all-out effort to stay at the surface by struggling, kicking and balancing; some of them have are in a wholly impossible situation, where thoy keep losing ground, whereas others do their best to slow down the process of sinking and desorgaruzation and stabilize a proletarian state at the low fixed income level.

"Thanks" to the very deep roots and highy refined culture or civilization of the decently poor city proletarian way of life, that state can be maintained even under extreme hardships. Offering as much energy for self-protection and reserve mobility in "hard times", that way of life exerts as great a retarding effect on social advance in "better times".

This also means that the present state of those people usually is the result of an advance made earlier out of a condition regarded as socially inferior to it (in our case, this being the "rootless" ); thus it represents not a stage in social ascent but rather means a fairly stable situation having a past but not much of a future. No great strides forward (in education, job promotion, financial conditions, home standards, ecological conditions) can be reckoned with.

Reflecting a lack of mobility background, the standard of education is characteristically low for this sub-category. Over and above the eight years of primary school (compulsory for everybody), which qualifies individuais for auxiliary and trained workers' jobs, mostly some kind of semi-skilled job is held by them (cook, waiter, storeman, dressmaker, hospital attendant, central heating fitter), chiefly of an auxiliary character. Such jobs are suitable mainly for establishing oneself in soma "small trade", through which onés head can be kept above water. The proletariaus on low fixed incomes could still hold their own (at that stage, if you like), and were not crippled, at least not to the extent of the rootless.

The simplest kind of offering help is "feeding" the needy within the family either over the week-ends or on work days, and handing on clothes to workmates or neighbours. Assigning these people into a separate sub-category is justified by the essential differences in the methods of earning their income. In short, as against the low fixed income proletarian, these people are effectively engaged in the second market or economy. That fact alone entails many factors that alter their ways of life. As preconditions, some marketable skills are needed, along with some useful personal ties and an ambition to get on in life; as consequences it should be mentioned that they can earn higher incomes, although taking higher risks and that their entire way of life is organized around their work or job; moreover, they tend to pick out jobs that promise financial advanta,ges over others,

Merely by way of making things clear, on establishing and delineating the different sub-sections or groups or groups seemingly incompatible aspects were used. A really simple method for establishing the characteristic qualities of welfare(?) proletariaus is to take a look at the families: wh~7e those of the present sub-category are basically the same types of proletarions they have been given or allotted council flats in the recent past, thus having received a significant welfare benefit. Wiewing things from perspectives which also being the organizing elements of the present, it can be stated that while the enterpreneurial and welfare(?) proletars did have a chance to get on, the low fixed income proletarians have a chance to survive, whereas the rootless have long lost all hopes of a better life.
