2006-Health of homeless EN

An Indolent Disease

The State of Health of the Homeless in Budapest

Péter Győri – Zoltán Gurály

Some of our questions asked on the 3rd of February 2002 were literally the same as those included in the National Public Health Survey (NPHS) of the year 2000. The latter was conducted by the Research Institute for Health Development and involved interviews with 5,500 individuals representing the country’s adult population not living in institutional households[1]. 

We adopted the questions in order to compare whether a lack of housing correlates with ill health. In selected parts of NPHS 2000, the state of health was assessed based on the answers given to questions related to well-being. The questions were very simple. They inquired if those interviewed were inhibited in carrying out their everyday activities by their physical condition.

We compared the answers given by homeless and non-homeless (interviewed in NPHS 2000) using sex and age-based classification.

With respect to homelessness, our primary focus is upon health-related problems that could be impediments to maintaining or obtaining a place to live.

The assessment of being hindered in everyday activities (such as getting up, lying down, standing, walking, etc.) does not differ in case of homeless and non-homeless people, since these are activities that everybody carries out the same way, at the same pace and with the same precision. Moreover, in case a person becomes incapable of doing one of the above to some extent, he or she, whether living on the street or in a house, will perceive it as being prevented. In addition to problems constituting inhibition, we also adopted a set of questions from NPHS 2000 assessing the rate of alcohol consumption and smoking. We inquired about the number of days spent in hospital and we have gathered data on the incidence of TB, skin diseases and frostbite. (The latter were not included in the questions of the national survey.) We also have answers regarding diagnosed diseases, but it is not possible for us to treat these in a simple enough way to mention them here.

We have become accustomed to the fact that almost everything perceived as bad appears more frequently in case of homeless people. We therefore expect to find that there are more ill people among the homeless, that there are more who complain and that homeless people spend more time in hospital. Also, that there are more smokers and there are more who consume alcohol more often and in larger quantities.

Broadly speaking, the results were as expected. That is, the answers received from those belonging to the group of homeless differ from the population’s average in a direction that indicates a disadvantage. However, the little differences and surprising parallels reveal much more subtle (and perhaps more important) connections than expected. In case of certain questions and certain groups of interviewees the answers given by homeless and non-homeless do not differ. Curiously, in some cases homeless people’s answers even appear more favorable.

Self-sufficiency

Homeless and non-homeless people have given surprisingly similar answers to questions related to self-sufficiency. During the homeless survey some who were being treated in facilities partly functioning as health care institutions (infirmaries, recovery wards) were also interviewed. The reason why these institutions were established and are still maintained is that traditional hospitals are unable or unwilling to receive homeless people. Nonetheless, only a little more than one-third of the homeless people complaining of problems related to self-sufficiency were staying in shelters providing care: 107 of them spent the night in a public place or mass shelter on February 3, 2002. Here, the question arises whether it is at all possible to live in a public place or mass shelter without the ability of self-sufficiency? (At the same time, more than two-thirds of those staying in infirmaries claimed to be self-sufficient.)

Walking

Answers, complaints related to walking occur more frequently with homeless people. Problems with walking may appear due to the homeless way of life. What we find, though, is that at old-age – over 65 – there is no difference between homeless and non-homeless people in the frequency of such complaints. That is, there must be the same proportion of people suffering from locomotion-related diseases among the elderly.

Eyesight

Every fourth individual and every second homeless person has a problem with their sight. In case of those under 35 years of age, the difference is not measurable, but in the elderly age group the disadvantage of homeless people is remarkable. As yet, we do not know exactly how this difference may be in connection with a homeless or non-homeless lifestyle. It could be nutrition or another lifestyle factor or simply the lack of “medical appliances,” of glasses, of treatment.

However, in case of those over 65, the difference changes in favor of homeless people: among those living in houses there are more who mention physical pain of some sort.

Mental state

There are more homeless than non-homeless people who complain of anxiety, which is not surprising. Regarding the mental or psychological plight, every third of non-homeless people complains of their state, and every second of the homeless. Is homelessness more of the origin of mental problems or does it imply mental rather than physical suffering? The frequency of the complaints related to the mental state of the non-homeless population of Budapest over 65 years of age “catches up” with the value measured among the homeless.

At this point we must ask: why does the frequency of the answers given by homeless people differ in another direction among those over 65 than in case of younger generations?

Only six percent of the homeless people are older than 65, whereas in the NPHS 2000 survey 18 percent of those interviewed belong to this age group. Among the homeless older than 65, the proportion of those over 80 years of age is only around five percent, whereas in NPHS 2000 this proportion is over 12 percent. Non-homeless are usually older and this may be the reason why diseases and complaints are more frequent among them. However, our more specific statistical examinations have proved that this difference in age distribution is only a very little part of the explanation.

As a summary based on our findings up to now we must say that the comparison of the state of health of homeless and non-homeless people has shown a smaller difference than expected.

The number of days spent in hospital

o the question “How many days did you spend in hospital as an in-patient the past 12 months?” 84 percent of the non-homeless in Budapest and 64 percent of the homeless answered none. The figures classified by age and by sex show the same: there are less homeless people who managed to avoid going to hospital than non-homeless. The difference between the groups compared is significant.

This is something we could not do, so – especially in case of the answers given by those surveyed at shelters – the results should be treated with certain reservations.

In the NPHS survey more than one question was included regarding alcohol consumption and based on those the individual was classified as abstinent, a social drinker or a heavy drinker. We could not apply such a precise method, so besides the frequency of alcohol consumption we could adopt only one set of questions related to the extent of consumption. Those who did not drink alcohol on any of the days examined easily can be called abstinent and those who consumed something each day of the week can be considered heavy drinkers. What we find is that among homeless people there are much more abstainers than what could be expected based on the average of the population. If we take the answers of those living and surveyed on the street, we find less of a difference, but the proportion of abstainers is still higher among homeless people.

However contradictory it is to the widespread prejudice about homeless people, we must accept the fact that among the homeless people interviewed at shelters and in public places of Budapest there are more non-drinkers than among the non-homeless. According to our findings it is also a fact that heavy drinkers make up the same proportion of the homeless as any other population. We may have lost one of our stereotypes here. (“A homeless is not the old boozy we all know by sight.”) Undoubtedly, the drunkenness of those without shelter is more visible and also more shocking if accompanied by the sight of untidiness or bad physical state.

The average rate of alcohol consumption among those interviewed in the NPHS survey is four glasses a week. In case of homeless people it is seven glasses a week. The majority of the homeless drink wine. The “disadvantage” of homeless people in the consumption of beer and spirit drinks is significant. The choice of beverage can be only partly explained by tradition. It is heavily dependent on financial factors. Therefore, should the amount of money spent on alcohol and not quantity be considered, homeless people will most likely not top the list.  (This assumption is also supported by the fact that at the time of asking, 41 percent of those living in public places had no money at all.)

However, based on the answers regarding smoking, we find that there are much more smokers among homeless than among non-homeless people. (Are smokes cheaper than booze? Or is it after all because of the possible problems with straight answers? As yet, we do not have the answers to these questions.)

Additional results regarding the state of health are asymmetric: we only know from the homeless people we interviewed that 13 percent of them currently have a wound or injury of some sort, nine percent have already had TB, 26 percent have undergone psychiatric treatment, 61 percent have had surgery and eight percent reported previous frostbite injuries. Non-homeless were not even asked such questions.

Those dealing with homeless people tend to paint a tragic picture of the state (of health) of these people. Visual media contribute a great deal to the conclusion of this image. Prejudices, which are of an averting nature and which suggest self-fault and again, the visual impressions of the street, establish a permanent link between homelessness and alcoholism. These links are undeniable, but their significance and reasoning value do not seem to be as solid as it appears. Especially if we acknowledge the fact that only a minority of those in a homeless situation are without a shelter, living on the street. The majority – with institutional or personal help – obtain a shelter and live a life less visible and less suitable for filming.

The greatest lesson, though, is that we were trying to find out how much worse the state of health of the homeless people was than that of the non-homeless and based on the analysis of the results we have found that the non-homeless population is also in an alarmingly poor state of health today in Hungary.
