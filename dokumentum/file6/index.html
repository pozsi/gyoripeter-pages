G l o b a l  p r o b l e m s,  E u r o p e a n  s o l u t i o n s

– First day of the conference –

        Friday was threatening us with getting corns on the bottom.  We were not scared away, sat it out, and it was worth the while. The first presentation was held by Zsolt Keserű under the title of „Introducing the Context: Integrated Approach”. Naturally, he was talking about the Hungarian situation, which we could quite well sympathise with, therefore I would not go into further details, I hope the presenter will not be offended.

        Linda Butcher was reviewing a European research called ”Multiple Disadvantages, Multiple Efforts”, which summed up experiences of the integrated approach in the social work of European countries. The programs and their realisation are similar within the transnational partnership, although there are some significant differences in the details. She was emphasising three key words: education, employment and homelessness, which, in the future, could be changing to full employment, economic development and inclusion of the needy.

        Although I liked the presentation, I was a little worried that the English lady will shortly invent scientific socialism, which us, older ones, knew long ago and thus could have been disputing her right to the invention, but fortunately this did not happen. Her ideas sounded somewhat familiar: government agencies should guarantee employment, control mitigation of differences between the sexes and insure catching up of disadvantaged groups. She emphasised that significant amount of money is needed for goals to be achieved. Defining poverty is the subject of great dispute, because it is not easy even to formulate it, not to mention drawing the dividing line between middle class, poor and needy.  In the United Kingdom, besides solving the unemployment problem of the homeless, unemployed and addicted people in a humane way, all people with disadvantages are meant to be integrated into society by enhancing their employability. She emphasised again: a lot of money is needed for this. In England, ten types of schemes have been worked out, and practice shows they work well if the disadvantages are moderate.

        Afterwards, participants of the conference asked questions and made comments relating to the presentation. The French delegates introduced the more specific services in Paris, which are more adjusted to multiple needs, require a great deal of creativity from support staff who are connected not only to local, but national politicians as well. Spanish delegates stated the question that if the problem and the solution are both global, than what at all is European about the issue? Linda, in her reply, said that it is not enough to deal with the problem in a meticulous way; a list of needs should be made which could be presented to the governments. In her opinion governments mean to do good by the policy of minimum wages (that all people, regardless of their ability to work, should have some income, a minimum value has to be set for pension, grant and support). She was also talking about the transnational scheme, which could not be realised for lack of financing; the profession is thus losing some of its services, its professional staff and their knowledge. She did not answer the questions; maybe she was distracted by the many comments. In my opinion, what is European in this program is that it is on this continent that we are trying to advance in improving the condition of the needy.

        The small-group discussions after lunch were not nursery level. The topic of the Hungarian group was ”What multiple hardships do homeless people have to face when finding employment?” It is really hard to list all barriers that a homeless person has to face when looking for a job. To overcome them is even harder. Those having a fictitious address have a great advantage over those not having one. Nevertheless, the main problem is the lack of dwelling places. As far as I know as much as 6000 special places (in hostels and shelters) have been created for those in need, while according to surveys there are about 6000-8000 (estimated value) homeless people in Budapest only. Consequently, at least 2000 people cannot get a bed in a service. Many say that they would not go to hostels even if they could (the case of the sour grapes?). Missing papers also balk employment; sometimes gathering them fails for amazingly minute reasons. A young man had lost his papers in the chaotic conditions at the time of losing his housing. Though homeless, he was able to find a job, and he was employed on condition that he gets his papers urgently, because his tax registry number is needed for his contract. He visited the local Excise Office to obtain his tax-card. He was there told – unrightfully, in my opinion – that he cannot get it without having an address-card. He could have then obtained a fictitious address, but the local municipality asked for his tax registry number for that (also unrightfully). Not to mention the fact that getting the papers requires money. Had there been no foundations, associations and other organisations, which help people out in such situations, it would be impossible to find a legal job for someone not having an address. These circumstances exclude them from the banking services as well. Even if one has been able to preserve his or her tax-card, found a job and made through the whole month, he or she still cannot take his earnings up unless the employer operates a pay-office. However, many entrepreneurs cannot afford that. You also have to pay for medical check-ups. If the employer can afford it, it will pay for the check-ups. In many cases, however, the cost is partly imposed on the employee. Naturally, the address card is needed for that, too. Further hardships include lack of reintegration, disfuncionality of institutions and legal unregulation. Long-term homelessness is a setback for people on the periphery. The more time one spends being homeless, the harder it is to get out of it. Those successfully reintegrated should continue to be followed by social workers; their support could prevent sinking back. At present, there is no time, money or energy for that. Employers are impossible to be tracked down by the social workers helping clients, thus supporting the client is really troublesome even in the rare cases when the relationship is preserved after the social situation of the client improves.

        The situation in Hungary is different from that of the Western European countries. In our country, it is primarily Hungarian citizens with Hungarian as their native language who fall out of the form of life accepted by society. Nations more fortunate than ours have to import their homeless from abroad, because national supply is so small.

        The three other participants of the transnational partnership struggle with problems of immigration. Earning an income is the main hardship for them, too, and it is even made harder by the fact that a significant part of immigrants do not get a work permit. These people can only be supported by being given beds in shelters and benefits, and there is a great chance that those becoming totally wrecked shortly criminalise.

        In all countries, there is an emphasis on personalised care, finding and maintaining a workplace that suits the person’s needs and skills. Speakers stressed the importance of motivation. A man hit by adversity becomes depressed, and most of the time medical care only treats the symptoms, but does not give strength and purpose to change the form of life. Recreational activities can help in that. Football has a significant role, and it is promoted by support organisations all over the world. In our previous issue we wrote about the Hungarian team taking part in the homeless world cup. The same way, teaching computer skills is also world-wide spread. It can be a recreational activity, because of computer games, as well as a useful skill, because being able to use the computer improves employability of homeless people. Stigma is a problem everywhere. Social judgement needs to be improved; the image of homeless people is far from reality. Only the top of the iceberg is seen, the few drunkard, junky, turbulent and littering ones, and people make their judgement of the more modest and quiet majority based upon them. The main problem, however, is that at no place has the whole range of needs been established (it is presently in progress in our country, too), which delays drawing up schemes. We can progress towards the solutions by continuing to involve users in the observational and preparational work. The conference had positive examples of that, too, which revealed that it is a motivational force for homeless people if their opinion is listened to, if their good suggestions are accepted, and if they are treated equally by the support staff.

M y  f e e l i n g s  o f  t h e  I n t e r n a t i o n a l   c o n f e r e n c e

        A was very much looking forward to the third international meeting of the EQUAL project held in Budapest. On my trip to Madrid half a year ago, I met several foreign social workers and I had come to like many of them.

        Unfortunately, I saw only a few familiar faces on the second day of the conference when I accompanied reporters from the foreign exchange groups to the restaurant near my hostel. The only person I had met before was Bettina Collins, who had been video-recording a lot in Madrid, too. She had been recording us all week, the result of which will be used for the film she is making. After lunch, I showed our foreign guests the outskirts of the Kőbánya-Kispest forest, where I had started my homeless life. Then at the bus stop they could catch an eye of homeless people taking a rest in the sun. In a short time we arrived at my hostel and I gave the delegates over to the director. Unfortunately, I had to hurry to the weekly team meeting of the editorial office of “Fedél nélkül”, thus I could not participate in the introduction of the hostel.

        On the next day, I met Bettina and the delegates again in the editorial office in Kürt street, they were being informed of the paper “Fedél nélkül” just then. After that, Bettina was shooting a video interview of the three supplement-writers for her film. At the end of the two-hour interview, we also had a chance to ask about the social situation in France, while I was recording Bettina.

        The rest of the delegates arrived on Thursday, among whom I had two acquaintances from Madrid – Carole from London and Maria from Paris – with whom I had been playing soccer at the time of the Madrid conference. The delegates then divided into four parts. I again accompanied a group to my hostel. The hostel was celebrating its fifth anniversary that day. On this occasion we organised a feast, the main attraction of which was users making bean-goulash in two stew-pots, which our guests did not even taste, unfortunately. I also tried not to overeat myself, because I was going to have dinner with the participants of the conference.  

        We introduced the first exhibition of “VAGYUNK” (“we exist”) - Association of Homeless Artists, a part of which was accommodated in the hostel.  During the discussions, an English lady handed us an informational brochure of a homeless art conference in London.

        I sat close to the English group in the pleasant grill restaurant, so – with the help of a translator – I was mainly communicating with them. After eating, the French and Spanish delegates started singing, which the English did not wish to join. Us, Hungarians, did not present a folk night, either, but we did sing the song “Száz forintnak ötven a fele”. I did it softly, so as not to chase the others out of the restaurant. Spirits were so high that it exceeded closing hours. The staff of the restaurant even used the music of “Silencium” to politely remind us, still we left half an hour too late.

        As for the seminar, an English and a French social worker had both purchased items from the works of the homeless artists exhibited, so some of the works of art may already have gotten abroad.

        Unfortunately, the French delegates were missing from the last night spent together on Saturday, because they flew home after the seminar. We went to “Gödör” in Erzsébet square, where we indulged in the enjoyment of music and dancing in great crowds. The topics of the discussions here were the evaluation of the Budapest conference and London plans for next April.

Erdős B. Gyula

E x p e r i e n c e s  o f  o u r  g u e s t s  i n  

B u d a p e s t

Our guests from the foreign exchange groups reported their experiences on the second day of the seminar. All Hungarian participants were looking forward to hearing the opinion of foreign professionals of the work they had seen in Hungary.

        From the 16th to the 18th of October, two professionals resided in Budapest from London, Madrid and Paris. This professional team had visited six social institutions in Budapest. On the 19th, the professional team had expanded to 26 members and visited four more sites. “SZIK” (Social Informational Centre) of BMSZKI and “Váltó-Ház” had been introduced twice. Our international partners shared the experiences of these field trips with the Hungarian professionals in the last hours of the seminar.

        The Jobseekers’ Office did not get great attention, because similar institutions operate in all three partner cities. Equipment and professional sophistication of the Hungarians do not fall short of those of our partners. On the contrary, it turned out that the ratio of those staff members having a degree in social sciences is greater in our country. The number of volunteers, however, is very little, especially in the actual work of institutions. It was already pointed out by our guests during the visit in the main department of BMSZKI that the flow of information is adequate among partners of the EQUAL project. However, legal representation in the London project is much stronger, as not only one lawyer, but a whole organisation supports the program.

        Contradicting views were stated concerning two practices of the “Kálvária” hostel of BMSZKI, where physically handicapped and chronically ill people live. They found the residence-time of maximally one year too long, and thought the practice of keeping medication locked away from sick habitants surprising, almost violating human rights. Hungarian professionals reinforced the necessity of the latter practice, referring to similar customs in Hungarian hospitals and the mental status of the clients. It was also mentioned that in foreign countries health-care and not the social sector treats sick clients.

        A few observations were reported concerning the day centre in Fehér köz, too. Providing coffee, tea and lunch, and the possibility to use the computer are common things to our partners, unlike everyday group sessions. French delegates found it an example to be followed that the day centre was open every day in the critical period, because in their country, there is no service on Sundays. The requirement of chest-X-ray and ÁNTSZ (National Medical Officer and Public Health Service) certificates (proving that the person does not have tuberculosis or any other contagious disease) for entering day centres was almost incomprehensible for them at first. Later, our guests realised that it was in the interest of other users that people with tuberculosis are not admitted to day centres.

        English professionals reported their experiences of the abstinent hostel called “Váltó-Ház”, similar to which was not mentioned in partner cities.  They found the use of the breathalyser and accommodating four people in one room peculiar. In their country, alcohol consumption is not forbidden in day centres for addicted people, and users have their own room and kitchenette in hostels. Lack of group sessions abroad was mentioned during the report about this hostel, too, as well as the length of the residence period. Our guests also noticed that only professionals are employed in the services; in their country a large number of volunteers are helping out continuously.

        I recognised one more interesting thing. Much to my surprise, in none of the three cities are self-help groups favoured, while they are strongly recommended by the support staff to addicted users of the Hungarian abstinent hostel.  

        After sharing their feelings of my dwelling place (“Váltó-Ház”), they did the same concerning my workplace (“Fedél nélkül”). Their most important experience was that we give numbers to the issues of the Hungarian street paper before giving it to distributors. This is something they would definitely like to recommend to the editors of the London street paper called The Big Issue. They said that distribution is controlled in their country, too, and the papers or the earnings can even be taken away, if somebody is doing his job wrongly. It was surprising to hear that the price of the London paper is one and a half pounds. Only “Fedél nélkül” has no fixed price in Europe.

        On the whole, social work in our country and the results achieved in the program were judged to be very high-level compared to the economic circumstances. They found many innovative elements in our project.

        To answer the question of what experiences they are taking home with themselves, our guests answered the following: “In the Hungarian EQUAL project, large-scale re-training of the workforce is well organised. The flow of information among the elements of the project is appropriate. Just like in other countries, the effectiveness of social work is largely influenced by the legal system and politics in Hungary. Appreciation and support of abstinence is of high quality in Hungary. Application of group sessions can sometimes even make up for financial deficiency. Exchange of professionals is to be improved for sharing such information.”

        In my opinion, EU money has helped to build up a social system that is progressing along the path of supporting homeless people, although it will never do away with homelessness.

        Finally, I gave my thanks that as a homeless person I could be an equal partner in the conference as well as in the professional seminar.

Erdős B. Gyula

I n t e r n a t i o n a l  C o n f e r e n c e  i n  

B u d a p e s t

The third international EQUAL meeting was held between the 19th and 21st of October in Budapest. The first delegates of the English, French and Spanish groups had arrived already on Monday. They discussed their program with the Hungarian partners, occupied their accommodation and visited several institutions of the Hungarian partner organisations. Some of these were also visited by the rest of the delegates arriving on Thursday. The three of us supplement writers accompanied the guests to the abstinent hostel in Vaspálya street, the day centre in Práter street and to the institution in Vajdahunyad street, which functions as a night shelter and a social home for the elderly, too.

        The SZIK (social informational centre) in the Dózsa György street building of BMSZKI was introduced by Zsuzsanna Molnár office-manager. She talked about how the office works, about the network of experts and the client turnover. She distributed an informational folder which, besides small presents, contained an informational package of the Hungarian service system. After that, staff of the Jobseekers’ office introduced their workplace. The delegates also met some clients. They could observe the services the office offered, and how they support those looking for a job. Our guests showed keen interest. After the visits in the institutions, participants of the conference took part in a dinner together.

        The conference started on Friday morning, in the auditorium of the Dózsa György street hostel. The exhibition of the works of homeless artists arranged there had a good influence on the atmosphere of the event. In the morning, there were presentations. Foreign partners reported of the social work, homeless and immigrant situation in their countries. After lunch, during the small group discussions, we tried to find solutions for the treatment of hardships, and answers for questions of being disadvantaged on the level of both the homeless people and the service providers. The rest of the afternoon was spent with the evaluation of group work and making comments.

        Saturday was the most interesting day for me. We were shown the international website, which – after continuous refreshing – makes it possible to keep track of the social work in the four countries of the partnership. After that, the foreign delegates arriving earlier shared their experiences. Participation was very active, there were many comments. From the overall picture it seems to me that the Hungarian system of services – which is only 16 years old –  contains many elements that our Western partners, having struggled with the issue for a much longer period of time, do not apply. One participant even noted that users are pampered in our country. In the concluding word, the colleague leading the conference asked us supplement writers, too, what experiences we were taking home with us.

        As for me, I had ambiguous emotions. On the one hand, it is “satisfaction”. If in nothing else, in the system of services we were able to catch up with Western standards. On the other hand, however, it does not give me pleasant feelings that at the beginning of the 21st century, poverty and homelessness are such emphatically present, not only in our country, but all over the world. (During one week I was participating in the second conference dealing with the issue of poverty.) I am an optimist. I wish that next time – in a relatively short period of time – we were getting together we could be reporting that the supply system is shrinking. There is no need for it! There is no replacement of the homeless and the poor. Due to the technical development, certain professions are so easy to acquire that finding a workplace does not mean a problem any more. No one has to choose between dwelling and eating! There is no dilemma of starving in my own flat or using the support system and have enough to eat. Those who need it – because they have not had the chance to learn it before – can get help from support people to develop the motivation to start controlling their life. They become able to decide what and how much they undertake. They can set and maintain their living standard themselves.

        Am I chasing clouds?

Szele Orsolya
